01 — The brief

You write the world you want.
CartoVox goes looking for it.

Most generators hand you the first random landmass and wish you luck. CartoVox reads a written brief, turns the requirements it can measure into checks, and scores reproducible seeds against them. If you ask for three continents and a candidate has two, it says two — it never quietly returns the nearest thing.

The reading never edits your brief. Each cue offers an explicit Apply; the rest stays for you to decide.

Try the idea

A toy search, drawn in your browser: six seeds, one hard constraint — exactly three continents.

    Press Search seeds. Matches are marked; misses state the actual count.

    This is a sketch of the workflow, not the simulator. The real search scores your whole brief against spherical worlds with moving plates — and the candidates below came from it.

    One thesis, four foundations

    The same sentence, four ways in.

    Every plate below was built from the thesis on the left. What differs is where the geography came from: two were searched from a seed, one borrowed an imported heightfield and let CartoVox complete the planet around it, and one took a coastline from an imported map and generated the rest. Each carries what it actually measured.

    Not one of these four answers the brief's three continents, and CartoVox says so rather than rounding. A miss is not a bad world — Amashima's single supercontinent is a fine planet, and an imported coastline is yours, not the brief's. It is a miss against this sentence, which is the only thing a search can honestly report.

    02 — The causal chain

    Every number in the dossier is measured, not invented.

    One seed and one configuration produce a spherical planet with moving plates and a real geological history. Everything downstream follows from the link before it — and you can click any cell of the map and ask why.

    Planet first, map second: the simulation runs on a sphere and is shown flat. East and west wrap; the poles are real poles.

    03 — One desk, three rooms

    Create the world. Explore it. Then write in it.

    CartoVox is a single desktop application with three workspaces that share one record. Rename a settlement once and it changes in the atlas, the dossier, the routes, the codex and every export — because there is only one place it lives.

    Four ways in, not four presets.

    • Generate — write the brief, preview seeds, shortlist, vary, then commit to a full simulation.
    • Import — bring a PNG, JPEG, TIFF or heightfield; CartoVox reads the coastline and simulates everything downstream.
    • Draw — paint continents on a wrapping ocean, carve rivers, write labels; the world is simulated on your sketch.
    • Deep time — assemble and break continents across hundreds of millions of years, then choose the era to live in.

    Before it starts, the preflight tells you the expected disk and time for your settings. Nothing is hidden in a spinner.

    Forty-nine views. One geography.

    • Map — relief, plates, climate, currents, soils, biomes, resources, settlement; click a cell and it explains itself.
    • Atlas — publication plates at up to 4800 px, labelled or not, with six cartographer reading desks.
    • Travel — route walkers, riders, caravans and ships between named places and read the cost of the road.
    • Campaign — spoiler-controlled player layers, saved regions and journeys, a presentation mode for the table.

    A Planet card shows the live globe; a Regional Lens turns any window into its own child map with inherited names.

    The book lives in the same vault as the map.

    • Codex — realms, cultures, trade, faith, places of power, and the measured questions the world raises.
    • Chronicle — a human-scale timeline you can author and play forward on the map, with consequences that persist.
    • Manuscript — a word processor inside the world: [[ links to any place, scenes on the map, checks that catch a distance or a date drifting.
    • Exports — Word, Markdown, Scrivener, EPUB, HTML; an Obsidian vault; realm handbooks; the whole worldbook.

    Generated lore is a proposal. Social history, politics and story hooks stay non-canon until you accept or rewrite them.

    For the table

    Then walk into one of its towns.

    Every settlement the world generated has a plan of its own, drawn from its site. Zhargundbar sits where a river meets the sea, so it has quays along the shore, a bridged avenue, a citadel on the high ground and seven quarters that are not interchangeable. Nothing about it was placed by hand.

    And in 0.8.3, shape it yourself.

    Settlement Studio plans villages and towns street-first: the street network is laid before anything else, the blocks those streets enclose become plots, and every building faces the street it sits on. The plan is chosen from the approach roads, water and relief the saved world already recorded.

    • Eight plans, not eight jitters — street village, green, crossroads, radial, organic, harbour, hilltop and twin neighbourhoods.
    • Quarters that differ — market, craft, townhouse, gentry, manor, temple, dockside, poor quarter and crofts each set their own plot size and building forms. Poor quarters deliberately do not line up.
    • Drawn, not stretched — roofs are built from each building's own footprint, so an L-shaped house gets a roof that fits. Fifty-seven pieces: eleven tree species, mills, forges, chapels, jetties, vineyards.
    • Redraw one quarter and every street and building outside it stays where it was.
    • Encounter scenes — standalone maps with no world at all, parent and child scenes with clickable hotspots, up to 32 lights, layers that blend and erase.

    Plans and scenes export as SVG, PNG and tiles. The whole of Settlement Studio.

    04 — The illustrated atlas

    Eight sheets of one world, drawn eight ways.

    Six cartographic traditions rather than six filters — ink on parchment with drawn profile ranges, painted relief in the Swiss school, a modern hypsometric atlas, a working nautical chart with rhumb webs and soundings, a sparse printable sheet, and a grimdark sheet where red is spent only on the blighted ground — plus two working bases: a realm map whose polities grow from their hearths by travel cost, and a clean coastline to colour on.

    With names, or without.

    Every illustrated sheet can be lettered, left as a painting base for your own artwork, or both — from the same source, without re-running the simulation. Rename a city later and redraw only the lettering.

    Named No labels

    Then zoom in, and it is still a map.

    Any region becomes its own plate with inherited names, local pins and hand-drawn routes. Original hand-drawn mountains, hills, volcanoes, trees, reeds, dunes and reef shallows are placed from the saved terrain, canopy and tectonics — the illustration never changes the geography underneath it.

    These eight sheets are drawn by the atlas. Render Studio is the other half: nine recipes of its own, with editable layer order, colours, line weights and label halos, plus contours, realm fills, compass, scale and vignette. Save a recipe, or share it as a small file. What Render Studio does.

    05 — The writing workspace

    A distance in your book cannot drift from the map.

    The manuscript editor lives inside the same vault as the lore. Type [[ and a name to link any settlement, river or realm. Pin a scene to the map. Ask what the weather was on the day the caravan left. The checks read your chapters against the world and tell you when a date, a name or a day's ride no longer agrees with it.

    • Names come from tongues — language families you define, so a coast sounds like its people.
    • History you can play — the Chronicle turns eras and events into a changing historical map.
    • Questions, measured — the Codex asks what the simulation cannot answer: who rules, who is allowed, what proves the right.
    • Leaves as a book — Word with real footnotes and tracked changes, Markdown, Scrivener, EPUB, HTML.

    06 — What it costs you

    The numbers most pages bury.

    CartoVox is a desktop instrument. It does real simulation on your own machine, and it tells you what that will take before it starts.

    Time

    Candidate previews are lightweight — a quick search takes about twenty seconds on a fast machine. A full build with every publication plate currently takes about 8 minutes on a high-end Apple Silicon Mac and about 13 minutes on a high-end Windows machine. The progress panel names the phase and plate and recalibrates its estimate as plates finish.

    Disk

    Budget 45–100 MiB for a Quick world, 180–360 MiB for a Full world without illustrated plates, and 337–883 MiB with every publication plate. The preflight reports the range and your free space, learns from your own finished builds, and warns before anything large.

    Machine

    macOS 12+ on Apple Silicon, Windows 10/11 x64, or a modern glibc Linux desktop. 8 GB of memory minimum; a full build peaks around 9 GiB on macOS, so 16 GB is comfortable. A keyboard and a precise pointer — the mobile layout is for reviewing a world, not building one.

    Honesty

    Every map view is classified: simulated field, deterministic derivative, proxy, heuristic, render-only detail, authored override or reversible scenario. The known limits — climate, erosion, isostasy, soils, society — are documented at length inside the application, before you build a story on a number.

    Your worlds are yours

    Publish it. Print it. Sell it.

    Everything CartoVox generates for you — worlds, maps, atlases, gazetteers, chronicles, codex vaults, names and manuscripts — belongs to you. Use it commercially with no royalty and no attribution. Your work is a file on your disk in an open, documented layout: an Obsidian-compatible vault, GeoJSON, SVG, OBJ and STL terrain, GeoTIFFs, and a scientific archive you can read with NumPy.

    No generative AI. Every place, name and event is derived from the simulation and the naming system. The same seed and the same settings give you the same world, every time, on every machine — which is the only kind of world you can defend in a sequel.

    Updates are your decision

    CartoVox can check once a day whether a newer version exists and will tell you when one does — installing it is a button you press. It never replaces itself, and the check sends no world or author data. You can turn it off and still ask on demand.

    Questions

    Before you ask on the server.

    Does CartoVox use generative AI?

    No. Names come from a language-aware naming system built on families you can define; every event, distance and number is derived from the simulation. It is reproducible: the same seed and settings give the same world on every machine.

    Does it need an internet connection or an account?

    Neither. CartoVox runs entirely on your computer with no account, no cloud service and no telemetry. The only optional network call is a once-a-day check for a newer version, which you can switch off.

    Can I start from a map I already have?

    Yes. Import a PNG, JPEG, TIFF or numeric heightfield and CartoVox reads the coastline — pale oceans, dark silhouettes, sepia maps and elevation maps are distinguished — then simulates climate, rivers, biomes and civilisation on it. You can also draw the coasts on a blank, wrapping canvas, or trace a reference image with magnetic outlines.

    Can I sell what I make with it?

    Yes. Worlds, maps, atlases, names, chronicles and manuscripts generated for you are yours, including for novels, game products, prints and commercial client work, with no royalty and no attribution. The application itself is proprietary and may not be redistributed; the full terms are in the licence.

    Is the simulation scientifically accurate?

    It is physically motivated and deliberately simplified. Plates, climate, currents, hydrology and soils follow a real causal chain and are checked against Earth-like bands, but CartoVox is a worldbuilding model, not a forecasting package. It documents its known limits at length inside the app.

    How big is a world on disk, and how long does it take?

    A Quick world is 45–100 MiB; a Full world with every publication plate can reach 883 MiB and takes about 8 minutes on a high-end Mac or 13 on a high-end Windows machine. The preflight tells you the exact estimate for your settings before anything starts, and you can build the plates later from saved data.

    Where is the manual?

    Inside the application, offline — every feature in the order the simulation produces it, the known limits, the licence and the third-party notices. The Help rail is one click from any workspace. The same material is on this site as the field guide, and every version's changes are in the release notes.

    How do I get in?

    Join the Discord server. Invitations, builds, checksums and feedback all happen there; a personal access key unlocks the app once, offline.
