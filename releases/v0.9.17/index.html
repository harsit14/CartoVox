CartoVox Delta V16

The last Delta. The whole app reads in German, French, Spanish, Portuguese and Polish, with numbers written each language's way. Villages, market towns and hill towns grow as they should, with greens, crofts, burgage plots, abbeys, landings and walls where history calls for them. Maps stay sharp however far you zoom, rivers run on across every join, and continent, region and disc maps open in the World map's own controls, with finer continent detail where you look. Orbits can wobble, swing their tilt and lock their spin, and a planet's real numbers build its real weather. Sculpt gains Overwrite, exact island shapes, Erode, River carve and Stamp, and deposits and goods can be edited by hand. Render Studio adds compass roses, scale bars, a cartouche, a legend, a neatline and your own symbol packs; Write adds Suggest mode, comments and an Outline with a Corkboard; campaigns gain templates, a combat tracker, dungeons from a sentence and Roll the road. Downloads go where you choose, and builds are faster: Quick about 30 seconds and Full about a minute and a half.

The application was called Atlas Studio until version 0.8.2. Builds are shared with invited testers on the Discord server; these notes are also readable offline inside the app.

Delta V16 is the sixteenth and last Delta release; the next release is CartoVox 1.0. Everything below is new or fixed since Delta V15, which was shared with invited testers on 7 November 2026. The whole interface is now in German, French, Spanish, Portuguese and Polish, with numbers written each language's way. Maps stay sharp however far you zoom, rivers run on across every join, and continent, region and disc maps share the World map's own controls. The city builder grows terrain-shaped towns, villages and great cities, and the tabletop, campaigns, the Compendium and Write each gain a round of tools. Orbits can wobble, swing their tilt and lock their spin, and a planet's real numbers now build its real weather. Deposits and goods can be edited by hand, islands can be drawn to an exact shape and overwrite the ground beneath them, and downloads go where you choose. Builds are faster: Quick about 30 seconds and Full about a minute and a half. Installed worlds need one whole rebuild after updating.

The city builder

  • Villages look like villages: cottages round the green and the church, farmhouses in hedged crofts at the edge, cottages along a river landing, a mill by the stream, and open fields farmed in long strips up to the backs of the crofts, in a large village as in a small one. The green is a proper open green by the village's middle, grass with a few old trees round its edge and a pond or a well (a well in desert or steppe), and the church stands in a churchyard on the street with the parson's croft and house behind it. A village no longer has a town's terraced rows or a row of warehouses on its quay.
  • A village's houses are cottages, each standing apart in its own garden, set back from the lane on a loose line and facing the streets and the green rather than the back lanes, instead of rows built wall to wall round every block; this holds in Settlement Studio too. Its crofts run long and narrow from the street right back to a back lane about 90 metres behind, with the farmhouse at the front, its barn across the yard behind it and often a byre down one side, and the blocks between the back lane and the next lane in, with no street along them, have their cottages along the back lane as well.
  • A hamlet of fewer than 100 people is a cluster of farmsteads along its road, each farmhouse with its barns in its own hedged close and a well by the lane, with no church, green or ring of lanes of its own.
  • A market town's high streets are lined with burgage plots, in Settlement Studio too: long narrow strips running back about 35 m from the street to the lane behind, a house wall to wall across the front of each and its yard and garden behind, instead of every block built round all four sides like a city's. The ground behind a deep block is built up in rows of town houses along the streets round the back, so a ring town's ring streets are lined with houses rather than garden walls. A town's houses stand on their street's building line, wall to wall along it and square to the street even on a plot cut on the slant at a block's corner, in a hill town's curving outer streets as in a market town's high street, and its three-cornered and pointed blocks are built up round every street they front, like its square ones. A manor, a trade yard or a smithy in a big block takes the end of it on its best street, with its house and yard, and the rest of the block is built up with houses round its streets, none left standing behind it with no street to reach them.
  • A market town's market place is a good-sized open place on its high street with a market house standing in it, rather than whatever block lay in the middle (on the coast, sometimes a square twenty metres across). A ring-plan town has one open square inside its own street, with a row of houses round it facing onto it and only the main streets running in, and a crossroads town's roads run straight into its market place as its main streets. A market town where three or more roads meet holds its fair in a Fair Field beside one of its roads out: open grass with booths along its sides and pens for livestock at the far end.
  • A market town has its coaching inns on the high street, by the market and where the roads come in: each a broad front range with the carriage way through it and a long stable range down one side of the yard behind.
  • A capital now has its castle. In a city the realm's seat raises a concentric castle and a lesser seat a stone keep; a capital of 30,000 or fewer planned as one town has one sized to it: a motte over a hamlet, a stone keep over a village or market town, a concentric castle over a seat of 12,000 or more. It stands on the most defensible ground by the old town or at the town's edge (a hill, a river bend, the high point), with its keep, gatehouse and ditch, a road from its gate into the streets, its name beside its walls and a number in the Places key. A hill town's castle takes the hilltop, on its highest ground inside its streets and wall, with a ring street round its green and the market just below its gate. In a walled town the town wall is carried round it, and the district's name moves off it.
  • A castle's keep is a great square tower with a turret at each corner, or a round great tower in a concentric castle; its wall towers are D-shaped, standing out from the curtain; its gatehouse stands between two drum towers; and close up their roofs are drawn behind battlements. In Delta V15 keeps were plain boxes and towers plain circles on the wall line.
  • A town from your world is walled only where its size or history warrants it: a realm's capital, a town of 5,000 people or more, or a town the world's history says was besieged, sacked, raided or fought over. Its town wall is drawn, with towers, and gatehouses where the roads pass through, standing just off the edge of its houses and close round them rather than taking in empty ground, with the way along it a street's width; an old town that is only a strip along its high road, as on a canyon's floor, has no wall round it. A village asked for walls, a frontier village round its fort among them, is closed with a timber palisade round its crofts, joined to its castle and gated where its road crosses. In Delta V15 only a castle's walls were drawn.
  • A walled town by a river is walled round the bank it stands on, the river its defence on the other side and a gate tower at the town end of its bridge, or round both banks if it is built on both. Where a wall meets the river it stops on each bank at a broad tower with a chain hung across the water between them, in the plan while you edit it, the tilted view and every export, keyed as "Water gates and chains". In Delta V15 a wall you drew across a river ran straight over the water. A lake town's wall stops at a broad tower on each shore instead of running on through the water, with a chain between them only where it crosses a narrow neck; its quay runs only where the town fronts its lake, and a quay with no houses behind it has no jetties or boats along it. A wall that only runs along a bank is drawn whole, as before.
  • A port planned on blank ground has a harbour with its moles, docks along the shore and a warehouse quarter behind them; in Delta V15 a blank coast read as marsh and a coastal city had no port. A port or capital on the sea has a harbour as large as its trade: a basin a few hundred metres across closed by two stone moles, a lighthouse on the head of the longer one, stone piers out into the basin with ships tied up along them, and warehouses on the streets behind its quays. A port on a long coast builds its harbour by the town, at a river's mouth where it stands at one, its quay breaking at the river rather than running across it, and a port at the head of a fjord has its harbour in the middle of the head, below its market. Quays with bollards run along the whole waterfront of a port's docks, warehouses and old town (about 1,550 m on a coastal city of 40,000, where it had about 450 m), and timber jetties run out every thirty-odd metres with boats tied up at them. A river port's jetties reach a third of the way across the river at most.
  • A city's harbour has boats: fishing boats at its jetties, boats and masted ships moored along its quays and ships riding at anchor in the basin, more of them in a larger port, in every style. In Delta V15 the water held one plain hull beside every other jetty.
  • The city builder's Water choice now offers Island: the town stands on an island big enough to hold it, built down to the water all round, a quay and strand all the way round its shore with blocks of houses facing it, and the sea on every side but one, where a causeway carries its road along its middle from the mainland to the town. In Delta V15 a town on an island drawn by hand stood in fields with its shore well beyond its last houses.
  • A town can stand by a salt flat: choose Salt flat from the Water list of a new town, or draw one with the Water tool, and a world town beside a salt pan or a seasonal salt lake has its own. The flat is drawn as white crust with a faint shore and, close up, its cracked plates; the town is built round it and its roads may run across it, no jetty, quay or boat comes to it, no field is laid on it, and its salt pans are cut in the crust at its edge with the salt house on the shore beside them. In Delta V15 the nearest a map-maker could come was a lake, with its jetties and quays, standing in for the salt.
  • The city builder's illustrated sheet now reads as a finished map: it carries the city's title cartouche, a map key whose swatches are the sheet's own colours, a scale bar, a compass, the numbered places when you ask for them, and the names of its streets, quarters, water and gates lettered in the sheet's own style, kept clear of each other, the key and the town walls. The export dialog's "On the map" choices apply to it too. Before, the illustrated sheet had no words on it at all.
  • The map can number the city's sights. Choose Places over the map (or tick "Numbered places" when you export) and the landmarks you placed, the squares, the market place, the largest churches and temples, the castle, the gates, harbours and bridges get a small numbered disc, with a key down the left side listing them by name, numbered north to south. A figure that would sit on a town wall, a gate or a street stands beside it on clear ground, with a fine line back to its place where it has moved far. Below the key, where the page has room, an inset plan of the city's greatest church or temple is drawn large in its own frame with its own scale bar, on the map and in every export. In Delta V15 only some churches and gates were named, and only close up.
  • Two new PDF exports: "City key" prints the plan with a lettered and numbered grid and its numbered places, then pages listing the places with their squares, each district with its people and trades, the town's services, inns, smithies and notable people (for a world's city) and its history era by era; "Street directory" prints the plan with its grid and then every named street in alphabetical order with the squares it passes through.
  • An illustrated PNG or SVG of a whole city of up to a million people now exports, the PNG at up to 16,384 pixels a side and sharp to its last row. In Delta V15 the PNG stopped at 8,192 pixels, and a city of more than about 250,000 people was too much for one sheet.
  • The city builder is friendlier on first use. A new blank city starts as a market town of 3,000 people, planned in moments, with a Market town choice beside Village and Town and the size button matching the population marked; the larger sizes are a click away, and a world's town still opens at its own size (in Delta V15 a blank city started at 200,000 people). Before a city exists, the right-hand column is a short card saying what you can do there, with Village, Market town, Town and City buttons that plan one of that size in a click, rather than an empty inspector and history, and the blank map says how to begin. The work under way (planning, filling districts, naming) shows on the map itself rather than only in a status line below the window's edge, the export dialog starts at a detail that suits the city's size (a whole city comes out at most 8,192 pixels a side rather than always one metre a pixel), the button says Generate castle when you plan a castle on its own, and a 3D model or GeoJSON export no longer says it is "drawn in the Plan style".
  • The map's own strip of choices sits above a city rather than over its top and keeps to one row: the season, the sun and the tilted view are under a More button beside the style, map key, places and export. Before, the strip wrapped to two rows over the top of the map in a laptop's window.
  • The map can be drawn in any season: a Season choice beside Style draws the sheet in spring (fresh green fields, orchards in blossom), summer (as before), autumn (russet woods, stubble fields) or winter (snow on the ground, yards and roofs, bare trees, frozen ponds and small lakes), in every style but the survey and the flat palettes, by night as well as by day. Exports are drawn in the season shown.
  • The city view has a Sun setting beside its style: light the city from any of eight sides, or draw it with no shadows. The roofs, the shadows of houses and trees and the hill shading all follow it, in the exports and the tilted view too. In Delta V15 every city was lit from the north-west.
  • The Night style draws a city by lamplight: dark roofs under a moonlit sky, about two windows in five lit along each house, a lamp in a pool of light every few doors down the main streets, and from afar the town as its lights. In Delta V15 Night was a dark palette with nothing lit.
  • Every city map style letters in the app's own faces, the same on a Mac, on Windows and on Linux, on the map, in its PNG and SVG exports and in the tilted view: the painted styles and the night in a book serif with its own italic for the water, the engraved and ink-and-wash styles with inscribed capitals for their quarters and titles, the survey in a clean grotesque, the tabletop in a face made to be read at a glance, and the Old map in the app's own hand-lettered italic. In Delta V15 the styles borrowed Georgia, Palatino, Helvetica or whichever calligraphic font the computer had, which a Linux computer does not have, so one plan lettered differently on each. The Old map style draws a city in sepia ink and washes on aged paper and frames the sheet in a decorative border with corner brackets and lozenges, in the view, the tilted view and every export.
  • The fields round a city are farmed as its climate farms them: vineyards in their rows and wide-spaced olive groves among the strips in a warm land, rice paddies in their bunds and plantations in the tropics, a belt of irrigated gardens and staggered date palms round a desert town with open desert beyond, open unhedged strips and grazing on the steppe, and high in the mountains or in cold country (5 degrees or colder, or above 1,600 metres unless the place is warm) rough grazing round a few small fields with no orchards. In the frozen north, where no crop ripens, a town has its herds' paddocks and kitchen gardens by its houses and the open tundra beyond, with no hedges, fields or woods. The open country takes the climate's colour. A world's city is farmed as its own climate farms it; a city on blank ground has a Farming choice of the same kinds. In Delta V15 every city had the same temperate strips, hedges and woods.
  • A city's trades keep their own quarters, as in real towns: tanners and dyers at the water's edge downstream (on a river, a coast or a lake), smiths and wheelwrights just inside the gates and where the high roads come in, the butchers' shambles a few streets from the market square and the guild halls round it, each with its own colour, plots, buildings and line in the map key. They follow what the market sells: tanyards only where hides, furs or dyes come to market, more smithies where metal is worked, shambles where cattle are sold, more guild halls for its crafts. Its workshop quarter, the craft rows with the guild halls, smithies, shambles and tanyards among them, is named and labelled as one quarter in the town's tongue. A town planned whole on its world's ground finds its own sea or river.
  • A town whose market sells what is dug from the ground has its workings on the slopes near it: adits driven into the hillsides and shafts with headframes, each with its spoil heap and a track down to the town, and, where it sells metals, an ore yard at the town's edge with a smelter or a stamp mill; a village or small town that deals in metals, a mining camp among them, has its smithies by the road in. A town whose market sells salt has its salt pans: a block of shallow pans drying white on level ground beside the town, by the shore of its sea or salt lake where it has one, with the salt house beside them and a track to the town. The workings and pans are named on the map close up (Silver Adit, Iron Shaft, Smelter, Stamp Mill, Salt House) and have their own entries in the map's key and the illustrated sheet's key; a GeoJSON export lists every adit, shaft, spoil heap, ore yard, mill, salt pan, salt house and track, and an SVG export is grouped into layers (ground, fields, water, streets, buildings, names and so on), with the workings and salt flats each picked out together. In Delta V15 a mining camp showed no trace of its silver, iron and lead.
  • A district you paint as an old town, slums or a craft quarter is planned as one: an old town in close rows of tall town houses, slums crowded and organic, and a craft quarter of workshops with smithies and guild halls among its houses, named as a quarter of rows and yards. Before, those three choices were quietly ignored and the city picked a type of its own for the district.
  • A city opened from the World map has a history: where the world's Chronicle or Living History says when the place was founded, How the city grew dates each of its eras from that year to the present, says that the old town stands where the place was first settled, and lists the events of the Chronicle that happened there in each era (a siege, a great fire, a charter). In Delta V15 the eras had names but no years.
  • A village within a day's ride of its faith's holy site (the sacred site the world's lore names) has an abbey just outside it: a walled precinct with its abbey church, the cloister round its garth, an infirmary, a great barn and a gatehouse, a lane to the village, and the abbey's own name on its precinct.
  • A city's old town is built in continuous terraces of town houses, each running wall to wall across its plot, the corners of the blocks built and every street of a block lined, so the heart of a large city reads as its densest ground. In Delta V15 houses stood as separate boxes with open ground between them, and whole sides of some blocks stood empty.
  • A city's squares take the shape of the quarter they open in. An old town's market place is an irregular, many-sided place where its streets cross, a wedge where a street forks or a long street market, with a fountain or a market cross in the middle, and a planned quarter (a civic, college or wealthy quarter, or one planned residential quarter in two) has a rectangular square laid out square to its streets; the squares you add take the same outline. An old quarter's blocks come in every size and few are rectangles, while a planned quarter's are rectangles of a few sizes. In Delta V15 a city's market was a ruled rectangle of bare paving.
  • Trees stand in the yards behind many of a city's houses (most of a manor's or a gentleman's garden, few of the old town's crowded rows) and in rows down both sides of its avenues, the ring roads where a wall was taken down and its high roads out through the newer town. In Delta V15 the only trees in town were in its parks, churchyards and orchards.
  • A city's roofs follow the same climate as its fields: high in the mountains or in cold country they are slate and shingle with little tile or thatch, among vineyards and olive groves mostly clay tile, in the tropics tile at the heart of the town and thatch round it, and in the desert flat. They lie in runs, a block mostly of one material with the odd exception, each material in three weathered tones, instead of tile and slate alternating house by house in one flat colour each.
  • A desert town is built as the dry lands build: its houses roofed flat (its churches and castle keep their own roofs), its yards, courtyards and open ground packed earth and sand on every sheet style, its greens and churchyards beaten earth, its gardens plots, and its yard trees a few date palms, most of them near water. A steppe town's ground is drier too. In Delta V15, on the greener styles, a desert town's yards were green.
  • A village on a hill winds up it in one street, from where the road arrives round the hill to a green and the church on the top, with its cottages along the street on both sides and stepped lanes running straight up the slope between its turns, and its road no longer runs through houses. In Delta V15 a hill village was a hill town's rings with the road zigzagging across them through the gardens.
  • A town of up to about 5,000 people on a hill winds up it in one main street, from where the road arrives round the hill to its market place on the top with the church beside it, with back streets along the hillside between the turns and stepped lanes straight up the slope. Its street climbs no steeper than about 1 in 25, turning more times on a steeper hill. In Delta V15 its road zigzagged up through the houses at up to 1 in 4.
  • A hill town of more than about 5,000 people keeps its ring streets round the hill, now running smooth rather than as a saw edge near the top, and its main streets wind up from the top out past the town's edge, crossing every ring street, no steeper than 1 in 12. The roads in arrive at its outer ring, and every one of them climbs within what the ground allows. In Delta V15 the road in was laid in ramps over the blocks between the rings, drawn as a field track across the town, at up to 1 in 6.
  • A hill town planned on open ground stands on its hill on the sheet too: the hill its streets wind up, and the slope of its country, are drawn in the relief, the contours and the woods on its flanks. A town high in the mountains, a small upland town or a city of tens of thousands, stands among them: mountain sides rise round it past its edge, opening into valleys where its roads come in and its water runs, its streets and quarters are laid out exactly as on level ground, and its country is rough grazing and a few small fields, terraced where the ground slopes. A town on ground you have drawn, a volcano's flank or a wide valley, has its map laid round the town rather than squeezed against one edge. In Delta V15 a mountain town's sheet was a level plain.
  • A village on a lake or by the sea is built down to its water: its lakeside lane runs along the shore with houses and gardens on that side too, instead of the village standing back across a field. A fishing village has a landing for every 160 or so of its people (two to five) along its strand, each with a slipway, a short stage with boats tied up and a few more drawn up on the shore, rather than a port's row of jetties. This holds in Settlement Studio too.
  • A town by a shore you drew is built down to it: the shore stays where you drew it and the town comes to the water, facing it and spread along it, its streets meeting the sea or lake at quays and a strand. A town (not a village) runs along its drawn shore the whole width of the town, its waterfront quays and strand all the way, and a town with people enough to line the shore of a small island you drew stands in the island's middle and rings it, its waterfront built all round, its market and church at the heart. The sheet is laid round the town itself and no more than a quarter longer than it is wide, so a long port town along its shore no longer sits as a small patch of fields in one corner of bare ground or on a strip with its countryside sheared off, but has fields and roads running on either side. In Delta V15 a town of 2,500 could stand more than 200 m back from its drawn coast.
  • A village or town in the forest stands in its clearing: its fields end at a ragged edge of woods running on to the edge of the sheet, with a few small fields cut out of the woods beyond, and a jungle river town stands in its clearing in the jungle with its plantations and paddies round it. On open ground, choose Farming › Fields cleared from the forest; a world's village or town in thick woodland gets it from its world.
  • A town on a river stands on that river where the map draws it, on its bank, and the river through the town follows the map's own bends, in the city builder and Settlement Studio alike. In Delta V15 a river town was planned from the middle of its map square, so the drawn river (meandering, or along a line you drew) often ran kilometres away or off the sheet. A river you draw on past the shore ends at its mouth, closing on the shore, its channel cut only as far as the sea or lake. Once you have built its city, the World map draws the town's dot where the city stands, on its river at the market, rather than at the middle of its map square; the place's own position is unchanged.
  • A town with a river drawn close past its market is planned on both banks, with bridges, at the size you asked for, rather than as a few streets on one bank, and a town whose road comes in over its river from the far bank grows on both banks, with a bridge between its two halves and the road running on beyond. A city on both banks is bridged in proportion to its size, about one bridge for every 20,000 people, spaced along its main river near the middle of the city, each with its high road on into the quarters either side, instead of every road from the far bank coming back over a single bridge; two high roads that reach a channel close together share one bridge, the second running along the bank to it, instead of two side by side 63 m apart.
  • Every town now has roads leading out over its fields to the edge of the sheet, bending with the land, never across its water, and drawn to read at any zoom. A city planned by districts runs its high roads on past its gates as country roads; a village or town on blank ground, in the city builder or Settlement Studio, has two from a village, three from a town and four from a larger one, with its fields laid along them. A road over the river by the bridge or in along the bank runs on too, a bridge that lands on an empty far bank has a road on over the fields to the edge of the map and no wharf along that bank, and a town whose plan kept none of its roads has them on the bearings it was given. In Delta V15 a town stood in its fields with no road leading anywhere.
  • A town opened in the city builder from the World map has a road out wherever the World map's roads arrive: each world road leaves the town on its own bearing, crossing a river on a bridge where one stands in its way, and a town whose roads come in from opposite sides keeps both. In Delta V15 a capital hemmed in by its river could lose the roads from that side.
  • A world's town where the world's main roads meet, or where a road crosses its river, opens in the city builder larger than the World map's figure (up to three tenths larger) and built up the more at its crossing, and the place's line says so; the World map's own figure is unchanged. Before, every town was planned at exactly the World map's figure, whatever roads ran through it.
  • A town or village on two roads leaving in opposite directions is laid out along its one road through, as a street town or a street village lying along its road (in Settlement Studio too), instead of as a crossroads or standing broadside to it; two roads meeting at an angle still make a crossroads.
  • A great city's quarters now meet along their streets, with no long, straight empty strips across them where no road runs. Its small triangles of ground where high roads, ring roads and river meet are paved squares or churchyards in the old town and greens or gardens further out, instead of bare ground (about a hectare and a half round a capital's castle). Its cemetery is a few hectares at its edge, its park a few hectares of its newer ground and its farm district a few fields and farmsteads on its rim; an outer district too large for any of them is built up with houses instead.
  • A city's squares, greens and churchyards are named and listed as what they are: the city key, the district list, the inspector and the GeoJSON and illustrated exports call them a town square, a town green or a churchyard rather than a park, name them as such in the town's tongue and count them apart from its districts, and a churchyard or cemetery is drawn on the illustrated sheet rather than left blank.
  • A city on a braided river draws it as one: its threads part round gravel bars and grown-over islands in its channel, with nothing built on them, and a town or village keeps them as a city does. A river that runs in several channels, as in a delta or a fen, is drawn so, its channels parting round long islands grown over with reed and willow. In Delta V15 a city of 60,000 on a braided river had one smooth channel.
  • A city's streets are drawn at widths that follow their rank: high roads widest, a medieval high street's twelve metres rather than the sixteen-metre boulevards of Delta V15, then main streets, streets, lanes and the narrow alleys behind the houses, the same on the map, in every export and in the tilted view, and a street's details name its rank. A town's or a quarter's main street is named as a street (a High Street, a Market Street, a Gate), avenues being left to a city's own broad high roads.
  • A city's streets and quarters each have a name of their own until you name them, fitted to the ground. A street called a Hill climbs one, a Harbour, Pier or Anchor street is by the sea or a lake, a Ferry or Weir street by water, and a street along the waterfront is a Quay or a Wharf; a large city names its second Bell Lane Little or Back Bell Lane. A quarter is a Marsh only on low ground by water, a Brook, Willow or Heron by water, a Harbour or Pier by the sea or a lake, a Hill, Heights or Mount only on a slope or the town's high ground, and a Strand, Quay, Wharf or Docks only by the water; each quarter takes a beginning of its own as far as the names allow, and only one is a Minster. The names hold on the map, in the street index and in every export.
  • A village or town comes much closer to the number of people you asked for: a small one whose plan could not hold them fills out with gardens and orchards at its edge, a river village or town no longer loses a whole quarter where a short stretch of lane met another street at the water's edge, and a town opened in the city builder houses all its people, its houses a little fuller where its plan came out a few houses short. Across 120 villages and towns on every kind of water the worst now holds 85 of every 100 asked for, against 53 in Delta V15. When a plan still holds noticeably fewer, the city builder says so ("Planned 148 of the 180 people asked for"), so you know to ask again.
  • A town in a canyon houses all its people: its floor between steep sides is built on, and the people a narrow stretch of floor has no room for live in the nearest quarter with room, and stay there when you redraw or regenerate another part of the town. When a district you plan again with a new seed has room for fewer people than before, the city builder says how many are left without a home and offers "Rebalance the town", which in one click moves them into the nearest districts with room to spare, leaving every other district exactly as it was; nothing moves until you ask.

  • An SVG map from Inkscape, Illustrator, Affinity or any vector export can be chosen or dropped as the main map. It is drawn at up to 4,096 pixels and read like a picture: shapes on a transparent page become your land.

Editing a built world

  • New Sculptor brushes, each with its own key. Erode (G): the world's own rivers and rain wear the ground you paint, with the same erosion a build runs and the water of the whole catchment above, so valleys deepen where rivers run and slopes soften into them; only the ground under the brush changes, and the change shows a moment after you lift the brush, exactly as the rebuild will make it. Wear (E) wears crests and sharp ridges down and lets a little settle in the hollows below, so new-looking ground reads older and softer without losing its valleys. Smooth coast (O) smooths a ragged shoreline without flattening the hills behind it or the sea floor in front. Wear and Smooth coast work on continent and region maps too.
  • A new River carve brush (key U): draw a line and it carves a valley along it and sends a river down it. The ground is lowered under the brush, the coast stays put, and the line becomes a river line, so every rebuild keeps the river there wherever the water can run downhill. Removing the river line from Edits takes its valley too, and Undo takes both back.
  • The Sculptor has a Stamp tool on the World map and on continent and region maps: it sets a whole landform down at once (a volcano, caldera, crater, mesa, butte, fjord trough, rift valley, atoll ring, meteor scar, ridge spur, dune field or glacial cirque) or your own greyscale image (white is the full height, black none; large images are reduced to 128 pixels a side). Choose its size, turn, height, edge hardness (faded into the ground or set down with a crisp rim) and how it meets the ground (add, raise to, lower to, replace, or only its raising or cutting part), tick Dig instead to sink it into the ground, and see it on the map under the pointer before you click; with Mirror on, its reflection is turned as well as moved, so a ridge spur, a fjord or your own image comes back facing the mirrored way. Each stamp is one pending edit you can undo, and Apply rebuilds only the area it reaches where it can. Flatten, and a stamp set to Raise to, Lower to or Replace, come out of a quick local rebuild exactly as they would from a whole rebuild, instead of standing 15 to 40 metres apart depending on which rebuild ran.
  • Mirror: choose Mirror › Across the equator, or across the meridian in the middle of the map, and every stroke is painted again on the other side, shown as a dashed ring under the brush while you aim. It works for every Sculptor tool that draws a shape, not only the painted brushes: a peak, a ridge, a range, a plateau, a basin, a valley, a crater, a drawn or round island, a filled polygon, a protected or preserved area, and a described region (its mirror image is read again for the place it lands in). A built island and a moved island are not mirrored. A mirrored River carve line carves its own valley and gets its own river. Each pair is one step for Undo.
  • Continent and region maps have Mirror too: a painted stroke, a range or an island is made again across the equator or the map's middle meridian, and Undo takes both back. If the mirror image falls off the map, only the stroke you painted is kept, and the panel says so.
  • On a continent or region map, the relief changes under the brush as you paint: the ground you raise, lower, smooth or flatten is redrawn at once with its new light and shadow, a hatch marking where it changed, and new land or sea where it crosses the shore. A Mountains stroke keeps the texture it showed while you dragged, and a crater, a described area or an island you pick up with Move island shows the moment you click. A "Preview of your edits" note above the map key and scale says it is a preview until you apply, and undoing a pending stroke takes it off the map straight away.
  • Steady hand: a setting on every Sculptor brush (Off, Light, Strong) that lets the brush trail the pointer on a short leash, so a shaky mouse still paints a smooth coast or ridge. The stroke still ends exactly where you let go.
  • Saved brushes: "Save this brush" keeps the Sculptor's current brush (its tool, size, height and settings, Mirror and Steady hand) as a chip above the brush settings, on every world; chips say "mirrored" or "steady" when they use them. Click a chip to paint with that brush again; its × removes it. Up to twelve are kept.
  • Story pins and working-map pins can wear one of 42 new pin icons (castle, tower, skull, crossed swords, tavern, temple, ship, cave, star, question mark and more) in a colour you pick: choose it when adding a story pin, or change it any time from the pin's panel or the working map's pin list, without a rebuild.
  • You can change the world's mineral, gem and fuel deposits yourself. Open a deposit's card in the Deposits layer and choose Edit to give it another kind, a name, a grade and a size (Rich, Large and the rest), a status (worked, or known but not worked), notes, or hide it from your players; Move to put it where you next click on the map; or Remove to take it away (it is listed under Removed in the Deposits panel, ready to restore). Add a deposit, in the Deposits panel or from the cell inspector, places a new one where you click. Only oil and gas may lie under the shallow shelf sea and everything else lies on land: a deposit added on the shelf starts as oil, a click in the open ocean is refused at once with that rule so you can click again nearer the coast, and a refused save says why next to the Save button. This works on the World map and on continent and region maps in every projection, every change can be undone, and each is kept as one of the world's revisions. The deposit form's close button sits in its corner.
  • Deposit kinds and market goods of your own. Make a kind such as dragonglass, spice or horses, with a group, a pin shape and, if you like, the market good it sells as. In the Deposits panel, give a good a name, a market group, the unit it is sold by and its price where it is made, and say which deposits or living things yield it (Ctrl-click, Cmd-click on a Mac, picks several); saving it says it is one of your market goods, or warns that no market sells it until a deposit kind or a species yields it. Markets near a worked deposit or a native species carry it and price it along their roads like any other good, by its own unit ("per blade") and in the realm's coins, on inn cards and in every export, so a dragonglass mine near a town sells dragonglass. A worked deposit you set or add puts its goods in the nearest town's market at once, and its Compendium page, landmarks and exports (GeoJSON, KML, the Obsidian vault) follow; one you hide from your players stays out of player handouts, vaults and atlases until a campaign reveals its mine, and its market names the goods but never the mine.
  • Your deposit changes survive rebuilds. A deposit the rebuilt rock moves a little keeps your changes; one whose spot the new rock turned into another mineral keeps your name, grade and notes there, listed in the Deposits panel with one click to make the edit name the new mineral; and one a rebuild takes away is listed under Edits that lost their deposit, where you can keep it as a deposit of your own where it was or let it go. The rebuild's report lists both kinds. If the file holding your deposit changes is damaged, the map, the markets, handouts and exports keep working with the deposits as the rock placed them, the Deposits panel says what happened and how to mend it, and nothing overwrites the file until it is mended.
  • Sculpting near the sea no longer rebuilds the whole world just because the coast moves: a coast moved a little, a bay cut, a shore built out or a beach lowered is rebuilt around the edit like any inland edit. Raising a new island, joining or splitting landmasses, opening or closing a strait, moving a long stretch of coast, or closing a small bay off from the sea or opening a hollow to it still rebuilds the whole world, and the review says which.
  • Sculpting ground under ice, or ground the last ice age's glaciers wore, now rebuilds just the area around the edit; only ice-worn ground within about 200 km of a fjord the glaciers cut still rebuilds in full. The ice and the glaciers' wear on the edited ground come out within metres of a whole rebuild.
  • A stroke painted inside a small region now builds again only the finest tiles it reaches and the rivers it turns, not the whole region: a small stroke near the edge of a 50-mile region updates in about 5 seconds instead of 11, and the rest of the region stays exactly as it was. On continent and region maps, a nearby edit that only nudges how rugged the ground reads rebuilds only the parts it reaches, not the whole map.
  • Move island now picks up a landmass that reaches all the way round the world, a band round the planet or a cap over a pole, and moves it north, south, east or west; moving it east or west keeps it exactly. Picking one up says plainly that it moves only those ways and cannot be turned.
  • A moved island takes its own rocks and mineral deposits with it, on the World map and on continent and region maps alike, so a volcanic island stays volcanic wherever you set it down. Kept areas and built ground on it go with it and keep holding their ground at the new place, drawn where the island took them; walls, roads and railways drawn across it go too, and so do hollows of dry ground below sea level, which also pick the island up when you press on them.

  • After a local rebuild, a realm keeps the towns its provinces were seated in, even where the edit moves or renumbers a seat town, and its counties, baronies and manors keep their seats wherever they still stand on its ground, so province borders stay where they were away from the edit instead of the whole province being divided again; roads away from the edit keep their rank (trunk road, spur or track).

In Delta V15 such a rebuild could end a realm that still held a few percent of the land, found a new one elsewhere under the old realm's name, or move a capital to another continent. A realm whose capital is gone takes the strongest town left on its own land as its new capital, and the rebuild log names any realm it could not keep and why: its land is gone, no town is left on it, or its capital now stands with another realm's. After any rebuild, the log's realm lines name a realm that ended or began as gone or new, instead of reading a new realm in an old one's place as that realm's territory collapsing.

Campaigns and the tabletop

  • The world now moves with the campaign clock, the same way every time, from its own wars, weather, harvests and bestiary. Realms at war send word of fighting from their border, storms strike towns, and heatwaves and cold snaps grip whole regions; each campaign year has its own weather, so the Today card's storms change from year to year. Every market gathers a harvest that a heatwave, a cold snap, a storm or a war can make poor or ruin, your creatures are seen near the towns in their range, and battles you apply in the battle simulator become news. Once a campaign is open, the Today card's news, prices and rumours come back almost at once.
  • News travels: a town hears of a distant event only when a courier could carry it there, by land or by sea. Beside the Today card you see what the party's town has heard, how long each piece took to arrive and the word still on its way, and the World map's place card shows what any town has heard by the campaign's day. Players see only what you tell them: click "Tell the players" on a piece of news to share it, and player mode (in each player's own language) and the player handout show the news you shared and nothing else.
  • Borders you redraw in a campaign change the world around the table. A town inside a territory change belongs to the realm you gave it to, for its news, its harvests and its market's prices. Taking towns from a realm starts a war with it, which ends half a year after the conquest, or sooner if you hand the land back, and a realm that has lost every town fights no more. Conquests, hand-backs and the end of a war all become news. A port is blockaded only while an enemy fleet lies within 150 km of it (your campaign's own fleets where you have placed them, otherwise the fleets' harbours in the world's military lore), not just because its realm is at war; a fleet token placed by hand sails for the realm you choose under Sails for, and until you choose, it says that a fleet of no realm blockades no one.
  • Market prices move with the seasons and with events: grain is cheapest just after the harvest, prices climb after a poor or failed harvest, a blockaded port pays more for imported goods, and a realm at war pays more for horses, iron and timber. The Today card names the goods whose prices moved and why, in the realm's own coins, and "Download today's price list" saves the whole list for the current campaign day.
  • Rumours come from the world as it stands: the news a town has heard, the price of bread, its realm's wars, upcoming feasts, the creatures in the surrounding country and a storm on the way.
  • When you start a session, a "Since the last session" summary says what changed: the days that passed, what happened in the world, the news that reached the party, the feasts held, the clocks the world moved, the price of grain and the quests opened or closed.
  • The Campaign desk has an End session button while a session is in play. It asks what happened, the rewards and the next hooks, closes the session on today's date (keeping a snapshot of what the party knows and moving the clocks that tick at a session's end), and shows what you wrote in the session's entry in Sessions, in its downloadable recap and under "Last time" on the next session sheet. It then offers Plan the next session, which puts the next session on the campaign board's Next session lane with the hooks and the secrets still to find. Start the next session still ends a session in play by itself, as before.
  • "Write the campaign into the Chronicle", beside the Today card's news, turns your play into history: one Chronicle era named for the campaign, holding every battle you applied, every border you redrew and every piece of news you told the players, each dated in the world's calendar and placed on the map. Writing again adds only what is new and keeps any edits you made in the Chronicle, and the Chronicle's Undo takes a writing back.
  • A world with no campaign offers five starting points: a sandbox hexcrawl from a frontier town, intrigue in a capital, a quest across a realm, a sea voyage between two ports and a one-shot by a place of power. Each is built from the world itself: the party starts at a real town, the first session comes with an opening scene and secrets about that town, the quests come from the world's story hooks, wars, creatures and places of power, and the clock starts in the right season in the local people's calendar. A Sandbox hexcrawl begins as its introduction says, the players' map ending at the town: they are shown the towns, ports, markets and places of power, but the fog hides everything more than 60 km from where the party stands, so no name beyond the frontier reaches them, while your own campaign map names every place, fading those the players don't know yet, with a key under the map saying how many. Once made it is an ordinary campaign, and all of it can be edited. Before the first campaign the starter campaigns show whole above the empty map frame, and the Campaign tab no longer keeps asking for them on a world that has none to offer yet.
  • The campaign's Today is a card of its own at the top of At the table, from the campaign's first day: its date in the campaign's calendar, today's festivals and those in the week ahead, and the weather where the party token stands on the clock's day (sky, temperature, wind, storm warnings and a seven-day outlook that counts only those seven days, a week at one temperature saying it once rather than "25 °C to 25 °C"), or, until a token is placed, a line saying how to get it. Its "Heard in" town has a Services and townsfolk button, so the inns, their bills of fare, innkeepers and rumours of the town where the party stands are one click away. Tick Players see today's weather to put the day's weather in player view and the player handout.

The living world

  • Every town, regional centre and capital on the World map now has its own population, worked out from the farmland around it, its market, its harbour or river and, for a capital, its court, so big cities and small market towns fall away smoothly from the largest down instead of every town being the same size. Settlement Studio plans each place at its own size, and a town's page in the World panel gives how many people live there and why it stands where it does: on a river or a great river, a sheltered harbour or the coast, a crossroads, fertile soil, the farmland that feeds it, and minerals nearby.
  • The road network is now a web instead of a tree of dead-end branches: towns are joined into loops, so the road between two towns rarely wanders far out of the way, and roads share stretches and bridges instead of running side by side. Roads are ranked by the traffic they carry between the places they join, so trunk roads run between the big, busy towns and quiet tracks reach the small ones.
  • Warm ocean currents along the western shores of the oceans, like the Gulf Stream and the Kuroshio, now carry their warmth much further. They no longer fade around 30–35°, where the trade winds turn into the westerlies; the sea beside them runs about 2–3 °C warmer than its latitude at 30–45°, against about 1.5 °C before, and their warm tongues reach well up the coast. The cold water off the western coasts of the continents stays cold, slightly colder than before.
  • The east coasts of the continents at 25–35° now get rain all year, as off China, the south-eastern United States and eastern South America. In summer the high-pressure cells over the subtropical oceans sink on their eastern side and send warm, moist air up these coasts; in winter, spring and autumn, storms form over the warm currents just offshore and bring their rain ashore. They now get about 1,200 mm a year at 30–35°, close to Earth's. West coasts at the same latitudes keep drier summers, so Mediterranean climates have a clear dry season and the coastal deserts stay dry, with a little more winter rain than before.
  • The storm belts of the mid-latitudes rain their air out more slowly and their storms carry on toward the poles, so the belt of heaviest rain is broader and less crowded at 40–45°, and the subpolar and polar lands get more rain and snow (a fifth to a third more poleward of 75°).
  • Coastal mountain ranges facing the westerlies are wetter on their seaward slopes: about a quarter more rain falls on them than in Delta V15, and it falls on the slopes themselves rather than on the sea just off the coast. The subtropical deserts stay as dry as before; the lands behind a second inland range get a little more rain, and the far north and south a little less.
  • Rainforest now evaporates at the rate measured over the Amazon, so the deep jungle keeps more of its rain for its rivers: about half of a rainforest's rain runs off, as on Earth, against two fifths before, and rainforest rivers run fuller. The deep interiors' rain is about 5% lower.
  • Sea ice in the year's weather now spreads and retreats like Earth's: the open sea must cool through the autumn before it can freeze, so the winter ice edge stops near 60° instead of reaching 50°, and the ice still grows thickest at the end of winter and shrinks back toward the poles in summer.
  • Land the last ice age's great ice sheets covered now lies a little lower, because the ground they pressed down is still rising back, as Hudson Bay and the Baltic are: up to about a hundred metres under the thickest old ice. Those lowlands hold more lakes, and their rivers find new courses through them.
  • Ice caps now begin where the Köppen map's ice-cap climate does: polar lowlands whose summers stay just below freezing are drawn as ice rather than green tundra, so the climate label and the land agree on every world, discs included.
  • The Weather year now plays over the World map itself: tick On the map in the Weather panel to lay the rain, temperature, wind or any other layer over the map, with the wind and current particles flowing across it and the storms drawn on their tracks, in whatever projection and turn the map is in.   • A lake on terraced ground that used to keep trading water with a hollow beside its shore, never quite settling, now fills that hollow first and becomes one steady lake.
  • Tropical storms come from sea that is warm for its own world, not from Earth's 26 °C: a world under a slightly fainter star now has its season of cyclones (in Delta V15 a star 6% dimmer than the Sun gave none at all), and a hotter greenhouse no longer doubles them. Worlds with Earth's star, air and greenhouse have exactly the storms they had.

Speed and memory

  • A new world is ready sooner: on the machine we measure on, a Quick build of the starter brief takes about 31 seconds against 46 in Delta V15, and a Full build about 96 seconds against about 2½ minutes. More of the build runs as compiled code (the land's noise, the climate's smoothing, rain, air mixing, its look-ahead over mountains, ocean-current tracing, the sweeps that find lakes and basins, and the fine arithmetic of every river bend, so rivers are drawn in about half the time). Roads, sea lanes and mountain-pass detours are found several times faster; landmasses, ranges and lakes are counted faster; the ocean currents map is drawn about three times faster; and city maps, coats of arms, the finished world's checks and the dunes' wind table are worked out alongside other steps. Every world comes out exactly as before.
  • A Full world needs about 2.5 GB less memory at its busiest, during the final climate (about 4.8 GB instead of 7.3 GB on the reference Mac), and builds a few seconds sooner; a Quick world needs about half a gigabyte less (under 2 GB instead of about 2.5 GB, and up to about 250 MB less again while its maps are drawn), and Low memory Quick about 1.5 GB. The worlds they build are exactly the same. On a computer short of memory, the work done beside a build (drawing city plans, checking the saved world, compressing the saved files) waits its turn instead of piling on top of it, so an 8 GB laptop is less likely to slow to a crawl or run out of memory; Low memory still does one thing at a time.

  • The Library no longer offers to adopt old folders from before worlds kept a save record; it lists only saved worlds.
  • Small fixes: deleting your newest world no longer moves the "New" stamp onto an older world; the Continue card shows the land's share of the surface in small print under its area instead of a stray "· 30%" on its own line; the "7 of 7 worlds" count sits on the same line as the search controls; after loading a world recipe the notice says "Complete" instead of "Nothing here yet"; opening a world while a large Compendium save is still running no longer fails to load its map hotspots; and a small world that takes a moment to open no longer says "A large world has many panels to fill".

Known limits

  • Before you update: this version makes the ground differently from earlier Deltas, so the first rebuild of a world one of them made solves the whole world once, however small the edit, and the rebuild review says why ("CartoVox was updated"). Edits after that rebuild only the area they reach, as before.
  • Finer continent detail builds a few tiles at a time: a screenful takes a few minutes on a Sketch world's continent.
  • A region's finest detail is drawn to match the 1 km cells the climate is solved on, not simulated; the click reading says which a spot is.
  • A Create candidate card can still show a thin land bridge or strait that the built world draws differently: the finished world solves its terrain at its own detail. Compare the candidates at the detail you will build to check before choosing.
  • Raising a new island, joining or splitting landmasses, opening or closing a strait or sea, closing a bay off from the sea or opening a hollow to it, moving a long stretch of coast, or reshaping ice-worn ground within about 200 km of a fjord still rebuilds the whole world. An edit on the World map near a small region rebuilds that whole region; strokes painted inside the region rebuild only the tiles they reach.
  • A landmass that circles the world moves only north, south, east or west, and cannot be turned.
  • A moon of a giant planet drifts nearer or farther through its Deep Time history, but its orbit does not grow rounder as a real one would.
  • Extra moons' tides are counted as if they all came into step at springs; how their months beat against each other is not followed.
  • Symbol packs stay on the computer they were imported on: a world or project backup opened elsewhere does not bring them along.
  • Zoom sharpening covers the ground maps, the political and culture maps, the planet's winds, currents, storms and ice, and the cartographer's styles.
  • On a continent map, the ground finer than its own cells is drawn, not simulated: a plain stays fairly smooth. Building Finer detail here gives real finer ground.
  • The full list, with what to do instead, is the Known limits page of the reference guide.
