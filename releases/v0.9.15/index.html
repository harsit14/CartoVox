CartoVox Delta V14

Mark where your mountains go, in Create before the world exists, on an import's guide page or with the Sculptor's Mountain guide, and the plates build real ranges there, with ridges, foothills and valleys. Imported plates can say which way they move, and the Sculptor's Plate motion tool turns a built world's plates. A region can be made from menus alone, with no description, and a wild region has no realm, no shires and no towns. A Polyhedral world map lays the world on an icosahedron, and Export makes it a printable hex sheet with glue tabs; Large map now exports the whole world too. Modern-day deposits such as lithium, rare earths and uranium join the resource maps. CartoVox gives memory back after each build, and Full worlds build about a fifth faster.

The application was called Atlas Studio until version 0.8.2. Builds are shared with invited testers on the Discord server; these notes are also readable offline inside the app.

All release notes

Delta V14 is the fourteenth Delta release. Everything below is new or fixed since Delta V13, which was shared with invited testers on 30 October 2026. A region can now be made from menus alone, with no description, and a wild region has no realm at all. You can mark where your mountains go, in Create before the world exists, on an import's guide page or with the Sculptor's Mountain guide, and the plates build real ranges there. Imported plates can say which way they move, and the Sculptor's Plate motion tool changes that on a built world. A Polyhedral world map lays the world on an icosahedron, ready to print with a hex grid. Modern-day deposits such as lithium, rare earths and uranium join the resource maps. CartoVox gives memory back after each build, and Full worlds build about a fifth faster.

Create

  • A region no longer needs a description. Its first step is now a set of menus with sensible defaults: climate, wetness, terrain, coast, water, how it is ruled, latitude band and the planet's look, each starting on Any (Earth-like for the planet). Pressing Find places with nothing chosen looks for good land anywhere. Your own words are still welcome, in a folded "Add your own words" box, and a menu wins where it and your words disagree. The sheet remembers your choices for next time, and the place cards' "Meets all you asked" checks list the options you picked, such as "Climate: Cold" and "Coast: Coastal".
  • The region's ruler follows the Polity menu: Kingdom, Duchy, Republic, Free cities or Tribes crowns a realm of that kind, and None/wild leaves the land without a crown of its own.
  • New words a region's description understands: hills, mixed terrain, moderate rain, lakes, and "island" for a single island ringed by sea (as against an archipelago). "Delta" now also asks for a coast.
  • The Sculptor has a Mountain guide tool for any world you create: paint where mountains go, as Hills, Mountains or High peaks at a width you choose, and Apply rebuilds the world from its plates with real ranges built along your marks and the plates' other ranges kept low. All your strokes are one edit in the list, Undo takes back the last stroke, and after the rebuild the tool says how much of the marked land stands above 1,500 m and names any marked stretch it could not honour, and why (over the sea, worn down, or too narrow for the world's grid).
  • A region with Polity set to None/wild is now truly wild land. No realm rules it, even where a realm of its planet covers the place, and nothing divides it into baronies or shires. None of the planet's towns or cities stand in it: at most a few scattered hamlets of herders and hunters, joined by tracks. Its overview, Library card, gazetteer, atlas and Travel all say it is wild land with no towns, and Travel plans journeys between its hamlets, or says plainly when there is nowhere to travel between.
  • When two choices on a region's sheet contradict each other, a short note under them now says which one wins and why, as you pick: "Water: Fjords needs a coast, so Coast: Inland is read as Coastal", "Wetness: Wet sets the rain, so Climate: Arid does not", or a latitude band against a climate's latitudes. A menu that overrides your own words says so too. Find places still works with any mix of choices. Fjords and Delta now win over Inland, since both need a coast.
  • You can now mark your mountains before the world exists. Shape of the land has a folded "Mark your mountains": it opens a blank sheet of the whole planet, with the chosen land shape's sample coast drawn faintly as a guide, where you paint belts of hills, mountains or high peaks at the width you choose, with Undo and Clear marks. Every candidate is built with mountains along your marks, and worlds whose land lies under them rank ahead of worlds that leave them at sea. Each card shows your belts over its map and how much of them falls on its land. The world you build raises its ranges there through the Mountain guide, keeps your marks in the Sculptor as its Mountain guide edit, and keeps the map's edges where the sheet had them, so the belts stand exactly where you drew them. With nothing marked, Create builds the same worlds as before.
  • A described area that asks for mountains or hills keeps the map's edges where you placed it too, so its range stands where the area is on the first build as on every rebuild.

Imported and drawn worlds

  • An imported plate map can now say which way its plates move. Under the plate map's card, Plate motion shows your plates where the world will hold them, each with a numbered dot: drag out of a dot to draw where that plate drifts, and the arrow's length is its speed in centimetres a year. Slow, Typical and Fast set Earth-like speeds of 2, 5 and 10 cm a year, Reverse turns a plate round, and a plate without an arrow keeps a simulated motion, so a map with no arrows builds exactly as before. The build turns each arrowed plate as drawn and its outlines stay as you drew them: ranges rise where plates now meet, rifts and ridges open where they part, and transform faults run where they slide past.
  • The page says in words what your arrows do along each shared edge, and warns plainly when two plates are pulled apart where your own map draws mountains, since the plates will open a rift there rather than raise that range.
  • A whole-world map with polar land along its top or bottom edge keeps that land when its coastline is read; before, a pale polar landmass was mistaken for a ruler or page strip and turned into sea.
  • A map inside a plain grey or light frame now has the frame cut away when its coastline is read, instead of the frame being read as sea and the whole map inside it as land.
  • An imported coastline whose land lay over open sea in the world its seed would have made no longer drains in long straight rivers. That ground used to borrow the sea floor's straight ridges and troughs, so rivers ran side by side in ruled lines and lakes formed in straight chains. It now gets rolling hills and basins that rise inland from your coast, so rivers branch and wind to the sea as they do on generated land. Your coastline stays exactly as drawn. Rebuilding an imported world may change its heights, rivers and lakes.
  • A new Mountain guide on the import's guide page says where your mountains go. Add a transparent picture with the mountains painted in (or dark marks on a light one), or press Paint the mountains and paint them over your own coastline, light strokes for hills, mid for mountains, dark for high peaks. CartoVox then builds its own ranges along your marks the way it builds its own mountain belts, with parallel ridges, foothills and valleys that the rivers, ice and weather wear and that cast rain shadows, rather than stamping a smooth hump. Elsewhere it keeps its own ranges low so they do not compete with yours, unless you tick Let CartoVox raise its own ranges elsewhere too. On a test coastline about nine tenths of the marked land stood above 1,500 m, against a sixth before, and the rest of the land above 1,500 m fell from about 15% to 5%. Your coastline never moves, and marks over the sea raise nothing.
  • A mountain guide from your import now stays with the world when you upgrade its detail, keep its current terrain, back it up, keep a previous version or save a rebuilt copy.

Editing a built world

  • A new Plate motion tool in the Sculptor shows the arrow every tectonic plate drifts along. Drag an arrow's head to change a plate's direction and speed, or pick Slow, Typical, Fast or Reverse. Each change is one edit that Undo, Disable and Remove treat like any other.
  • Applying a plate's new motion rebuilds the whole world from its plates, and the review says why: every boundary of that plate is read again, so mountains rise where plates now converge, rifts and ridges open where they part, and the ranges the old motion raised wear away. The plates, boundaries, bedrock, seismic hazard and volcanic maps follow. Your own sculpting and drawn ranges are laid back on top, and kept areas, towns and roads hold as in any rebuild.
  • A described region that asks for mountains, highlands, peaks or hills is now built by the plates through the mountain guide: a real range with ridges, foothills and valleys along the shape you drew, instead of a raised dome. The world's other mountains stay as they were. The region's ground-change strength sets how high they rise, and a strength too low for hills gives a gentle rise instead. Volcanoes, drowned land and the other described landforms work as before.

Maps and exports

  • A new Polyhedral (icosahedron) projection lays the world out on the twenty triangles of an icosahedron, five round each pole and ten round the equator: the classic hex-map world sheet of Traveller and other role- playing games. Every face keeps areas true. Choose it on the World map, where every look and overlay follows it, for atlas plates, or as a picture from Export.
  • Export › Polyhedral world map draws that sheet ready to print: a hex grid lined up with every triangle (5 to 30 hexes to an edge, with hex numbers if you want them), the climate bands named down the side at your world's own tilt, the names of continents, realms, seas and towns, a title, and glue tabs so you can cut it out and fold it into a globe. Draw the land from the map on screen or as vector fills, and save it as a PNG up to 8,192 pixels wide or as an SVG or PDF. Keep a corner on each pole, or turn the solid so that as little land as possible falls on the cut edges, as Fuller's Dymaxion map does.
  • The Resource prospectivity map now shows the deposits a modern-day world is read for: uranium, lithium (brine under dry salt flats and old pegmatite), rare earths, bauxite, phosphate, diamonds, and offshore oil and gas tinting the shallow shelf sea beside oil-bearing basins, rifts and great deltas. Each has its own colour in the key and can be shown on its own, on the world map and on continent and region maps, and each follows the rock that makes it: uranium in granite and the basins beside it, rare earths at rifts and mantle plumes in old crust, bauxite on hot, wet, stable ground, phosphate off cold upwelling coasts, and diamonds only on the oldest cratons. Towns, realms and names do not move: people were not drawn to uranium or bauxite before industry.
  • The Deposits pins add uranium mines, lithium brine fields and pegmatites, rare-earth mines (including the weathered granite clays of the wet tropics), bauxite mines and geothermal fields, each with the rock it sits in. Coal is also found in the basins at the foot of mountain belts and as peat in waterlogged lowlands, oil in rift basins, phosphate off cold upwelling coasts and potash in the brines of closed dry basins.
  • A place's dossier gives a line for each modern prospect under it, such as "diamonds at 50%: kimberlite country on an ancient craton". The new resource classes come out in the vector SVG and PDF exports, and the new pins in the GeoJSON and KML exports, with the rest.
  • On the all-resources map, lithium leads on the salt flats that hold it and rare earths at the heart of rift and hot-spot country, with copper and gold keeping the ground around them. Phosphate keeps to the coldest upwelling coasts, under 1% of the land. Towns stay where they were.
  • The World map in the Polyhedral projection now has a Turn choice: Pole up, or Fewest cuts, which turns the icosahedron so its cut edges cross as little of your world's land as possible. The turn is worked out for each world, kept with it and worked out again when a rebuild moves the land; borders, pins, measurements and the hover reading all follow it.
  • Export › Large map… now works for the whole world as well as continents and regions: any World map projection, the polyhedral sheet and its fewest-cuts turn included, at up to 16K, as PNG, PDF or SVG, with the coast, rivers, borders, a graticule and a title cut cleanly at every seam.

Speed and memory

  • CartoVox gives memory back. When a build, continent map, region map, large export or Render Studio render finishes, and whenever the app has had nothing to do for about a minute, it lets go of the maps and fields it was keeping for quick viewing; the next view reads them again. After a long session of builds, maps and exports it now settles a few hundred megabytes above what it used when it opened, where it used to keep over a gigabyte more, and building again and again does not add to it.
  • Region maps, continent maps, large exports and new worlds count the memory CartoVox can let go of as free when they check whether they fit, and let go of it before they start. You no longer have to quit and reopen CartoVox before a region map will start.
  • Full worlds build faster. Working out how the winds carry moisture and rain across the planet now uses more of your computer's cores at once, inside each season as well as across the four seasons. On the reference Mac the first climate pass of a Full world takes about 26 seconds instead of 46, the final one about 18 instead of 28, and a whole Full world is ready in about 2 minutes 20 seconds instead of 2 minutes 50. Quick worlds gain too. Every world comes out exactly the same as before, down to the last number. Low memory builds keep to one core here, as they do everywhere else.

Saving, updating and the Library

  • A Library card's buttons wrap onto a second row when they don't fit, instead of pushing Delete past the card's edge where it was cut off.
  • The Library's Continue band lays itself out by its own width. With a world open beside the Library, or in a smaller window, it squeezed the world's name and details into a sliver beside the map, and the details ran down the page a letter at a time; now the map gives way first and then moves under them.
