CartoVox Delta V15

A city builder: any town opens as a city on its real coast, rivers and hills, and grows from a village to a metropolis of two million, with walls, gates, docks, slums, suburbs and districts named in the world's own tongue. Paint districts, lock roads, add landmarks, tilt the city into 3D and export it in seventeen styles, including VTT battle maps; the World, continent and region maps show it as you left it. A world can be a flat disc with an Edge. The World map sharpens as you zoom, up to eight times finer, and straits and land bridges hold on every finer map. Nations can be given the languages they speak, islands can come from an SVG drawing, and Create gains Rules of the world. Rain reaches deep into continents and piles up against mountains, with sharper rain shadows; the Weather year adds heatwaves, gusts, the polar vortex, moving sea ice, icebergs and plankton blooms; every world has an impact history, and moons of gas giants get eclipses and tidal volcanoes.

The application was called Atlas Studio until version 0.8.2. Builds are shared with invited testers on the Discord server; these notes are also readable offline inside the app.

All release notes

Delta V15 is the fifteenth Delta release. Everything below is new or fixed since Delta V14, which was shared with invited testers on 5 November 2026. Its headline is a city builder: any town opens as a city on its real ground, growing from a village to a metropolis of two million with walls, docks, districts and names in its own tongue, and it can be tilted into a 3D view and seen on the world's maps as you left it. A world can now be a flat disc with an Edge. The World map sharpens as you zoom in, and a world's straits and land bridges hold on every finer map. Nations can be given the languages they speak, islands can come from an SVG drawing, and Create gains Rules of the world that change how winds, erosion, rivers and biomes work. Rain now reaches deep into continents and piles up against mountains, with sharper rain shadows behind them; lakes that meet at one level are one lake. The Weather year adds heatwaves, gusts, the polar vortex, moving sea ice, icebergs and plankton blooms, and every world has an impact history. Moons of gas giants get eclipses, the giant's light and tidal volcanoes.

The city builder

  • A new city builder replaces Settlement Studio, from a hamlet to a city of two million people. Open city on any settlement (its dossier, the World map, a continent or region map) opens it on its real ground; Make › Make a town starts from a world place or blank ground.
  • A city grows over the land it is given instead of being a disc cut by water: a town on a spit fills the spit, a delta city builds and bridges its islands, a valley town keeps to the valley floor and terraces its slopes, a bay city lines its shore with quays, a lake town reaches its island by causeway and a cliff town its harbour by stairs.
  • A city opened from a world stands on the finest ground the world has there (a region, continent or child map), with its coast, lakes and named rivers in place, the coast drawn as a smooth curve rather than a staircase of the map's cells. Asked for more people than that ground holds, a world's city spreads over the real land round its place, up to 40 km across and kept off sea, lakes and steep slopes, so a coastal capital asked for a million gets close to a million; what you drew and locked stays where it stands, and on a small island the builder says how many people the land holds. On blank ground you draw the ground first (coast, sea, land, islands, rivers, canals, hills, valleys, cliffs, contours or a heightmap) and the city is planned on it.
  • A large city is laid out as a metropolis: an old town, a wall and ring road for each walled age, high roads from the country, swallowed villages with their own centres, docks and warehouses, works, a well-off side, slums, parks, cemeteries and suburbs, each district with its own street pattern (grid, organic, radial, courtyard blocks, suburban lanes or streets along the contours), houses and people. Small towns are built of cottages and houses for a household or two.
  • Tools for city builders: paint sixteen kinds of district, draw and lock main roads, bridges and causeways, set wall circuits with gates and towers, add ages of growth, place squares and fourteen kinds of landmark, shape the ground, lock streets and buildings, regenerate one district. Every change is built at once and replans only the districts it reaches; the rest of the city stays exactly as it was. Sixty steps of undo, an inspector with figures for anything you pick, and one-key shortcuts.
  • Streets, quarters, gates, landmarks, bridges, squares and quays are named in the place's own tongue (with its stress and accent marks); names you write are kept. A city saved in a world appears in its dossier with its quarters, people, walls, gates and bridges, its named places become World panel entries, it is drawn on the World map at deep zoom, it follows its town through rebuilds and it travels in the world's backup.
  • Seventeen drawn styles, among them three new ones (Ink and wash, Atlas colour and Survey), with roofs of tile, slate, thatch, shingle, lead and copper by quarter, age and climate, shaded and casting shadows; cobbles, earth lanes, flagged squares, gardens; water with banks, quays, jetties and boats; parks, cemeteries, fields, hedges and woods round the town; walls with towers and gatehouses; hill shading, contours and cliff hachures, with hillsides that read as relief from afar. Zoomed out a city reads as a city map; zoomed in, as buildings and named streets. Map key adds a title, a key, a scale bar and a compass.
  • Exports: PNG at any size (tiled past 32,768 pixels), SVG, a PDF sheet or poster up to 4 × 4 sheets, GeoJSON, an illustrated sheet in thirteen styles, and Universal VTT battle maps with walls, doors and gates, all from one Export dialog. A million-person city exports as a PNG about 12,000 pixels across in a few seconds.
  • Everything Settlement Studio offered is here: castle and keep plans with a ditch or moat, Save as a scene and Step inside a building for furnished interiors, a growth view that plays the city through its ages with each age's walls, and food and water panels (fields needed and drawn, people fed, granaries and mills; springs, wells, cisterns, fountains, conduits and aqueducts) with map overlays.
  • A million-person city plans in under a second on a recent Mac and pans and zooms at the screen's full frame rate; regenerating a quarter takes about a third of a second, and the saved city is about 15 MB.
  • A scene made with Settlement Studio opens its town in the city builder, planned again from the scene's recipe; hand edits made to that old plan stay in the scene and are not carried into the city.
  • A new reference page, The city builder, walks through opening a city, drawing its ground, districts, roads, walls, castles, styles, exports, growth, food and water, and the builder's limits.
  • Tilted view: a city can be seen from an angle, its ground in relief and every building standing at its height, with its walls, towers and gatehouses, on a slab of cut ground. Drag the picture or use the arrow keys to turn and tilt it, raise the ground and the buildings, and save it as a PNG up to 4,096 pixels across. A metropolis of a million people opens whole in a fraction of a second, standing in its blocks; zoom in for the houses one by one.
  • A city you build or edit now shows on the world's maps as you left it: the town carries the city's name, and its mark grows with its people on the World map, continent maps and region maps, and on the World map a town grown to a metropolis draws larger than a capital. Zoomed in on a continent or region map, the city's built outline and its walls are drawn too.

Create

  • Create's Star & orbit panel now takes your own system's numbers in one place, with units: star mass and luminosity, orbital distance, eccentricity, year and day length, axial tilt, spin direction, where in the year the closest approach falls, and axial precession. As you type it shows what they make: the sunlight in W/m² and against Earth's, how much warmer or colder that makes the world, the sunlight at the closest and farthest points, which northern season the closest approach falls in, the year Kepler's law gives (and the year now follows the orbit and star mass here too, as it does in Advanced), and the star's type and temperature, with one click to use the brightness a normal star of that mass would have. Numbers that make a very hot or very cold world, pass the edge of a star's habitable zone, or would likely lock one face to the star are kept and come with a plain warning instead of being refused. Dim red dwarfs as faint as 0.0001 Suns (TRAPPIST-1 is 0.00055) are no longer raised to 0.001, and Deep Time takes the same star and orbit ranges. The closest approach and precession are specialist controls, held back in Essentials.
  • A precessing world is now an option: give the precession period in years and which year of the cycle to build, and the closest approach moves round the seasons accordingly. The world's calendar says how many years until the closest approach falls at northern midwinter, the equinoxes and midsummer.
  • A tongue's stress has more choices: first, second, second-to-last, third- to-last or last syllable, Latin-style by syllable weight (second-to-last when that syllable is long or closed, otherwise third-to-last), or free, where each word keeps a stress of its own. Under the choice one plain line says what it does, with a word from the tongue's own sample names showing the stressed syllable in capitals. The stress now holds for a tongue built only from flavours too, where before it was kept only once the tongue had letters of its own, and tongues led by the Greek flavour now really stress the third-to-last syllable in their pronunciation guides, as the flavour always said.
  • A new "Show stress with an accent mark" option on a tongue writes an acute on the stressed vowel of every name it gives (Vélarion, Viána) on the map, in the gazetteer, the Compendium and exports, while choosing exactly the same names as without it. The Syllable shapes field also offers a few ready-made sets (CV, CV CVC, (C)V(C) and more) to start from.
  • A region world can now have your own star and orbit: Create › A region has a folded "Star and orbit (optional)" section with the same star, orbit, year, day, tilt, closest approach and precession settings as a whole world, with the same live readings and warnings. The planet the places are found on is built with them, so a dim star finds cold places, and the region world's overview and Library card show the star and orbit it was built with. Left alone, region worlds are built exactly as before.
  • New way to begin: A disc world. Make one flat, round world with an Edge instead of a globe, from 2,000 to 7,500 km across, at Standard, High or Ultra detail. Choose where the sun goes (circling over the rim gives an icy hub, a warm rim, seasons and nights; standing over the hub gives a warm hub and a cold rim with no seasons and no night), how many climates the disc spans from hub to rim, and whether the sea pours over the Edge or a wall of ice stands there. Its climate, winds and rain run in rings round the hub, and its towns, roads, realms and names are made as on a region world. Words such as "an icy rim", "a wall of ice" or "the sea falls off the edge" choose these settings for you.
  • A description that asks for a flat world, a disc world or the edge of the world now offers to make a disc world from those words, instead of saying the maps can't show it.
  • A disc world reads as a disc everywhere you look: Find places, a place's card and the disc's GeoJSON give each place's distance in km from the hub and its bearing, never the hidden globe's degrees, and "nearest places" point the way on the map, with north at the top.
  • A disc world's GeoJSON holds the disc itself: its rim, provinces, towns and villages, roads and rivers on its flat plane in km from the hub, with a note in the file saying so.
  • A disc whose sun stands over the hub says so: no night side is drawn (it is always day, the light lower toward the rim), the weather names no season, and a journey's day is never cut short by nightfall. On every disc the weather's date and winds read on the disc, with no hemisphere.
  • On a disc world, the World map's tools and exports that would show the hidden globe behind it (turning the world, day and night, continents, the regional lens, world projections, globe prints, KML and map tiles) are put away, with a pointer to the disc's own Export and Atlas. Travel plans journeys on the disc only.
  • The hub of a disc draws cleanly: no star of streaks in the sea floor round it, and under a sun over the hub, no ring of bare mountains round a forested centre: the tree line now falls away from the hub as the warmth does.
  • Advanced settings have a new group, Rules of the world, for the settings that change how a world works rather than what it starts from: how hard the winds blow and how deep the wind belts run, how fast the land wears down, how hard rivers cut their valleys and how much more a great river carves than a stream, and where the biomes draw their lines (where dry country begins, the rainforest, savanna and temperate rainforest lines, and the tree line). Each has a clear range and its own explanation, and left alone they build exactly the world they built before.
  • A set of rules can be saved under a name and used again on a later world in one step (and undone in one step), exported as a small file, and imported from a file someone else shared.
  • Erosion rate now wears the slopes down as well as cutting the river valleys, as its description always said, so a world set to wear faster comes out with lower, rounder peaks. Worlds described as young or ancient show it too: young land keeps sharper relief, old land is worn lower.
  • A moon of a giant planet can now choose where its giant hangs: "Longitude under the host" in Planet & climate › Giant host puts the giant over any longitude of the equator, and the near side's eclipses, its light, its volcanoes and the dossier follow it. Left alone it stays over 0° N 0° E.
  • A moon in an eccentric orbit close to a heavy giant now has volcanoes, as Io does: the giant's tide heats its interior and raises extra volcanic chains along the equator either side of the giant's meridian and the one opposite. The Giant host card says how many. A circular orbit raises none.
  • A moon's tilt now wobbles over the long cycle: its orbit slowly turns round the giant, so its seasons strengthen and weaken over decades or centuries. The Giant host card gives the cycle's length and the tilt's range, and "Year of the precession cycle" picks where in it a world is built.
  • A disc world whose sun circles over the rim now lets you choose where its warmest ring falls: toward the rim (an icy hub, as before), two-thirds of the way out, halfway (a cold hub and a cold rim with a warm ring between them) or at the hub (a warm hub with seasons and nights). A second menu makes the warm band usual, narrow or wide, under either sun. The sunlight, the seasons, the rain belts and the tree line follow your choice, and the sheet says what the disc's climate becomes. Left alone, discs are made exactly as before.
  • Two or more lakes that fill to the same height and meet across flat ground at that height are now one lake, with one shoreline, one name and one outflow, instead of neighbours that passed water back and forth across the shared flat. Their rivers now settle on one course every build, even on very terraced or stepped ground, where some lakes used to be left part- filled and out of balance with their rivers. A typical world has a few such lakes, so it has slightly fewer lakes than before. Rebuilding a world may join lakes like these.
  • Rain now reaches deep into big continents. The trade winds carry the sea's moisture far inland instead of blowing in from the dry country beside the rain belt. Equatorial lowland basins 800 km or more from the sea are wetter (about 2,100 mm a year against 1,850 before), more of their rain comes from the sea instead of their own forests, and the wettest coasts are back to their old strength.
  • Low winds that meet a mountain range they cannot climb now slow down in front of it and turn to run along it. A lowland basin facing a high range gets the heavy rain that falls where the trades pile up against the mountains (one test world's basin went from about 1,700 to 2,000 mm a year), and the land behind most mid-latitude coastal ranges is a little drier against the windward side.
  • Equatorial land has a wetter dry season, closer to the Amazon and the Congo, and still one clearly drier time of year.
  • Rain shadows are much sharper. Air that crosses a high range now sinks and dries as it comes down the far side, and moist air climbing the windward slopes of the mid-latitude ranges rains on them. Behind high ranges facing the prevailing wind you now get dry steppe and desert, as in Patagonia behind the Andes or the Great Basin behind the Sierra Nevada. On the test worlds the windward side of these ranges now gets between 1.7 and 3.2 times the rain of the land behind them, against 1.3 to 2.4 before. Low hills barely change, the tropics and the great equatorial basins keep their rain, and a typical world has slightly more desert, most of it in the lee of mountains.

Imported and drawn worlds

  • Import or draw now takes a Photoshop .psd as your main file, chosen or dropped like any picture, so you no longer export each layer to PNG by hand. CartoVox lists the file's layers by name, each with a small picture, and suggests a part for each from its name: land or coast becomes the main map, and height, mountain, river, biome, Köppen and plate layers become the matching guides (English, German, French, Spanish, Portuguese and Polish names are recognised). Labels, frames, text and hidden layers are left out. Every suggestion is a menu you can change; a group can be used whole or layer by layer, and when no layer is named as land the whole flattened picture is the main map. The chosen layers line up exactly as they do in Photoshop, and from there the import is the same as one built from PNG files: the same coastline and guide reviews, and the world saves, rebuilds and backs up the same way. A .psd without layers imports as one flat picture. Pixels are read as painted, so rasterize layers with effects first; adjustment layers, blend modes and masks are not applied. 16- and 32-bit files and large documents (.psb) are refused with a note on what to save instead, and CMYK and Lab files are converted to RGB.
  • On the drawing desk, hand-drawn rivers, roads, towns and realm borders stop growing past twice the zoom, so deep in they no longer cover the ground you are drawing on.
  • On an imported or drawn world, every simulation-health warning now says whose ground it is about: the relief or coastline you supplied, ground CartoVox simulated around it, or partly both. Climate, biome and settlement warnings say they are about what CartoVox simulated on your ground. Only warnings in your own ground are listed last.

Editing a built world

  • The Sculptor's marks on the map stay sharp at any zoom, including over an HD region: pending-edit rings and numbered discs, the brush ring, Move island, Plate motion and Mountain guide marks, roads, river lines and the selected place were soft, oversized blobs when zoomed in and are now crisp lines with discs and labels that keep their size on screen.
  • On continent and region maps, a pending sculpt edit's change is drawn as clean cells under its outline when zoomed in, instead of a soft blur.
  • Add island has a Shape choice and a Crisp edge option. Besides the round island it can now make a ring with a lagoon in the middle, a polygon dragged out from its centre (untick Equilateral for a rectangle; four equal sides make a square) or a shape you draw by clicking its corners, with Shift snapping each side to 15°. Tick Crisp edge and the coast is exactly the shape you drew: a circle stays a clean circle and a rectangle keeps straight sides and sharp corners at every zoom, in HD regions, on continent and region maps and in exports, with no bays, headlands, spits or reefs worn into it, and it stays that way through every rebuild. Leave it unticked for the natural, wavy coast, which is still the default.
  • A new Climate panel (Tools → Simulation, or Sculpt → Terrain adjustments → Rain and warmth) changes a finished world's whole climate: a Rainfall slider from half as much rain to twice as much over the whole planet, and a Temperature slider from 10 °C colder to 10 °C warmer. Apply rebuilds the world from its climate on the terrain it already has, so mountains, coasts and valleys stay exactly as they are while rivers, lakes, forests, deserts, wetlands, ice and towns follow the new rain and warmth. When it finishes, the panel shows what changed, before and after: rainfall over the planet and over land, land temperature, the share of land under desert, forest, wetland, ice cap and lakes, and the river channels. Each change is a restore point in the world's history, and continent and region maps of the world are rebuilt on the new climate as after any other edit. For one area only, the panel's Describe a region button opens the Sculptor's tool for writing "desert", "rainforest", "steppe" or "tundra" into a drawn shape.
  • A global temperature change applied from the Climate Lab keeps the terrain too: the world is rebuilt from its climate onward, a little quicker than a whole rebuild.
  • Zoomed far in, the points and lines you edit with keep their size on screen: river line and road points and the lines between them, a placed or moved settlement's rings and name, the highlight under the pointer and the Nations brush ring stay a few pixels across instead of swelling to hundreds and hiding the ground. A town is grabbed from the same short distance at any zoom, so a click beside it reaches the ground.
  • Add island can raise islands from an SVG drawing. Pick Shape → From an SVG file and choose a drawing from Inkscape, Illustrator, Affinity or any other vector app. Every closed shape in it becomes an island: paths (each separate part of a path), rectangles, circles, ellipses and polygons, including those moved, scaled or rotated inside groups. The panel says how many islands it found and what it left out: a shape inside another (such as a lake), an open line (such as a river drawn as a stroke) and specks. Click the map to place the drawing at the width typed in Across (km), turned by Turn (°), or drag out from its centre to size and turn it with the pointer (Shift snaps the turn to 15°). Each shape is an ordinary drawn island, so Crisp edge, Undo, the Edits list and Apply treat it like one drawn by hand. Nothing in the file is run or kept: only the outlines are read.
  • You can now choose the language any nation speaks on a built world, not only the realms you placed in Create before building. Open Nations, select a nation and pick its language under Language: any built-in family or a tongue of your own. You can also give it a second language and choose how much of the country speaks it: a corner, a region or half. The second language takes the part of the country nearest to where it is spoken over the border, or the part farthest from the capital when no neighbour speaks it, so the map shows two peoples rather than a scatter. Apply redraws only the names, so the ground, climate, rivers, borders and towns stay as they are. The nation's name, its capital, its people and every generated name inside its borders (towns, rivers, ranges, deserts, lakes and natural regions) are redrawn in its languages. Names outside its borders and names you chose yourself stay as they are. The Languages map, the Compendium, the lexicon and finer continent and region maps all follow the new languages, and Undo in the waiting list takes a choice back.
  • The climate questions in the World overview's findings (ice on warm ground, rain with no traced source, seasons out of order, temperature or rainfall the causes do not explain) now have Show on map, which frames and outlines the cells around the worst place, and the rain and unexplained- climate questions have Explain this cell, which opens the cell inspector's climate explanation at that place.
  • The Planet Advisor's findings now match the World overview: Show me frames the whole place a warning is about, each warning says whose ground it is on or that it is about the whole world, and a setting repair offers Preview repair, which shows what would change before anything is rebuilt.
  • When a place's unexplained rain is both recycled over land and partly untraced, Explain this cell now links to each share on the moisture map, not only the first.
  • On a built disc world, the Climate panel can move the warmest ring and make the warm band narrower or wider. The terrain stays exactly as it is, and the climate, rivers, biomes and towns are worked out again for the new rings. Before, the only way to change these was to make the disc again. Under a sun that stands over the hub, the hub is always the warm ring, so only the band can change.

Maps and exports

  • The World map grid now goes down to 1° and 2° squares, with a heavier line every 10° and degree labels along the left and top of what you are looking at. Zoomed out, fine lines are thinned to a spacing you can still read and the grid says so, instead of turning the map grey.
  • The grid's new Lines menu marks your world's equator, tropics and polar circles, worked out from its own axial tilt (a world with no tilt is told it has none), and lets you add your own lines of latitude or longitude, such as 45° N or 30° W, each with an optional name and colour. Your lines are kept with the world, follow every projection including Polyhedral, show over an HD region, and appear in the vector SVG/PDF, the Large map and the map saved as shown whenever the grid or graticule is on. The menu also points to Measure distance for sizing an island.
  • The measuring line, its points and its distance labels stay sharp and keep their size on screen when you zoom in.
  • Landmarks and routes, Deposits pins and the numbered points on the map (such as the world's highest and deepest points) are now as sharp as the map at any zoom and over an HD region. Deposit pins and numbered points keep their size as you zoom in, instead of growing until they hid the ground they mark; roads, borders and landmarks keep the size they had.
  • Continent and region maps now have a grid of their own: tick Grid in the map's bar, or press the World map's Grid button while one is open. Its lines follow the map's own projection, thin out when zoomed out and carry degree labels where they enter the map, with a heavier line every 10° (every 1° on fine grids). A region is often less than a degree across, so it offers 0.1°, 0.25° and 0.5° squares and opens at a spacing that fits it; a continent offers everything from a quarter of a degree to 15°. The fixed 10° grid that used to be painted into the Albers, equirectangular and globe views is gone: those views now show the grid only when it is on, at the spacing you chose.
  • The Lines menu works on continent and region maps too, and the lines are the world's: a latitude or longitude you add there is added to the world, the lines you added on the World map show on its continents, and the tropics and polar circles come from the planet's tilt. A region world keeps its own lines.
  • Measure distance now works on continent and region maps. Click the map to set points; the path follows the curve of the planet and its length appears as you go. Undo, Clear and Done sit beside it, and Esc also stops. A measured path is not saved.
  • A Large map exported from a continent or region map while its grid is on now starts with the graticule ticked, uses your grid's spacing and draws your marked lines with their names. Degree labels on these exports now sit where the lines meet the edge of the picture, and a region with no grid chosen still gets more than one line.
  • A disc world's map is the disc itself, with nothing of a globe about it: its grid is rings round the hub, marked in km, and spokes by bearing; your own lines are rings at the distances you give; a click says how far from the hub a place is and on what bearing; the scale reads true across the disc; and large exports keep the Edge, the rings and a title box without degrees or a north arrow.
  • A small lake high in the mountains no longer floods the lowland below it on continent and region maps: each lake keeps to its own ground. Saved continent and region maps route their water again the next time they are brought up to date.
  • Disc worlds, region maps and continent maps no longer have single cells tens of kilometres deep or high beside a shallow shore. Where a fjord or drowned valley held most of a coastal square's sea, the rest of that square's depth used to land on one or two cells; it is now shared over the whole square, so the relief key, the highest peak and the deepest sea report real heights. Continent, region and child maps built before this read out of date and are built again.
  • Continent, region, disc and child maps no longer dig ocean trenches kilometres deeper than anything on the world beneath them. Their sea floor stays within a few hundred metres of the deepest part of the world nearby (up to about a kilometre beside a steep drop or a narrow trench, so the floor slopes smoothly off a shelf instead of stepping at the world's squares) and still averages out to the world's own depth, so the deepest point a relief key reports is close to the world's real trench. Low dry basins below sea level no longer hold lone pits 4 km deep or towers beside them on High and Ultra detail. Continent, region and child maps built before this read out of date and are built again.
  • In Sky & planet's star chart, the giant host's lit side now faces the sun.
  • On a disc world the World tools read the disc. Measure runs straight across the disc in its own kilometres, matching the scale bar. Explain climate explains the place you click on the disc, against the average at that distance from the hub. World facts keeps to the disc: nothing past the rim, places by distance and bearing from the hub, shares of the disc and the disc's own area, highest and deepest points. Show on map in World facts and Show me in the Advisor fly to the place on the disc. Tectonics, the Erosion, Coast and Climate Labs, Landmarks and Deposits, which only show the hidden globe behind the disc, are greyed out while the disc is open and say why and what to use instead.
  • Maps by question, at the top of the map layers, now opens on the question that fits what you were just doing: Sculpt and the terrain labs open Terrain & water, the climate tools Climate & seasons, Campaign and Travel open Travel & trade, the writing tabs Story & lore, and the Atlas or Render Studio a new Print & atlas question listing the illustrated world sheet, the styled sheets and the vector sheet. Pick another question (or close the list) and that choice is remembered for that kind of work. The maps you opened lately on this world are offered underneath, and only maps this world actually has are listed; a question it cannot answer is left out.
  • Zoom that keeps going: zoomed into the World map past the point where its cells show as blocks, the ground on screen sharpens by itself, with no rectangle to draw. Coasts, rivers and relief come in finer as you go in (up to eight times the world's own grid), drawn from a continent or region map you have generated where one covers the view and re-simulated like an HD region everywhere else. The map you had stays on screen until the finer ground is ready, a small pan keeps it, and ground you have already looked at comes back at once. Markers, labels, the grid and edit handles stay crisp on top of it, and the line at the top of the map says where the detail came from. The saved world is never changed.
  • View › Sharpen as I zoom turns it off (it is on to start with), and the HD region works as before. The real-detail card no longer has its Automatically switch; it offers "Show real detail" when sharpening is off.
  • An HD region's picture now lies exactly on its ground. A rectangle dragged across the middle of the world's cells could sit up to a cell off.
  • Sharpen as I zoom now works on the Biomes, Annual temperature and Annual rainfall maps as well as Physical relief. Zoomed in, biome edges follow the finer ground (tree lines up the valleys, forest giving way to alpine on the ridges), temperature follows every hill and valley, and rainfall shows the wet windward slopes, each in that map's own colours. The biome map's chosen classes and lakes are respected.
  • Where a generated continent or region map covers the view, its coast and its own rivers are now drawn as crisp lines over the sharpened ground at any zoom. Before, its coast went soft at the deepest zoom and its rivers were not shown at all.
  • Panning a sharpened view no longer reshapes the ground: neighbouring windows now draw the same hills, coves and biome edges where they overlap, and zooming from 2x to 4x to 8x adds detail without moving what was already there. The HD region's ground follows the same rule. Small rivers near a window's edge can still differ.
  • A narrow strait or land bridge on the World map stays a strait or a bridge on its continent, region and child maps and in the closer look: where two seas or two landmasses meet through a gap one or two world cells wide, the finer map keeps a channel of open water, or a neck of land, at least three of its own cells wide between them, so a voyage or a march that works on the World map works on every finer map too. Rebuild a continent, region or child map to see it.

Campaigns and the tabletop

  • On the Travel map, a journey's route, its start and end, the towns you can pick and the weather marks keep their size as you zoom in.
  • Journey weather on the Travel tab flags gusts on storm days and heatwave and cold-snap days along the way, and gives the journey's strongest gust. A journey saved to a campaign keeps all of these, and they appear in its downloaded weather timeline.

The living world

  • The year's length now shapes the seasons: a longer year gives the seas time to warm through summer and cool through winter, so its seasons swing wider, and a short year evens them out. Worlds with the default year are unchanged.
  • On an eccentric orbit, the calendar, the Weather year's dates, eclipse seasons and the sky view now put the closest approach in the same season the climate does. They had read it a quarter of a year early, so the Eccentric Orbit world's calendar made summer and autumn short when its climate gives a short spring and summer. Earth's numbers now give Earth's own season lengths.
  • Day and night and the Satellite look's night side now show a moon's giant at work: the day goes out while the giant eclipses the sun, and the near side's nights are lit by the giant's reflected light, brightest when it is full.
  • The Weather year marks a moon's eclipse seasons on its dates.
  • Day & night now works on a disc world. The sky explorer draws the disc itself with its Edge: the lit side sweeps round the hub with the hours, and the seasons move it toward the hub and away. Click the disc to stand somewhere on it; where you stand reads as a distance from the hub and a bearing. Under a sun that stands over the hub the explorer says plainly that it is always day there and how high the sun stands at the rim, and the map's night layer now draws that light dimming toward the rim instead of only describing it.
  • The Weather year has four new map layers: heatwave days a year, the hottest afternoon of a typical year, the gust a place sees about once a year, and the gust it sees about once a decade. Hover over the map to read them at any place.
  • Each year of weather now lists its heatwaves and winter cold snaps on their dates, as rings on the map and as lines in the log you can jump to. The summary names the year's hottest heatwave and its coldest cold snap.
  • The Weather year now follows the winter polar vortex in each hemisphere: how strong it is at each step, and when it weakens or breaks down. Cold snaps hit the mid-latitudes in the weeks after it weakens, most often in a hemisphere where continents and oceans stir the air.
  • Every storm now shows its strongest gusts, which are stronger ashore and on exposed summits than at sea.
  • Hovering over the Weather year's map shows the reading at the pointer again; the storm layer had been hiding it.
  • Continent and region maps show the year's heatwaves and cold snaps, and a place's weather reading now includes its heatwave days, its strongest gusts and any heat or cold spell that day.
  • Sea ice in the Weather year now grows and melts through the year from the world's seasons, so it lags the cold: it is widest after midwinter and least after midsummer, and ice that outlives the summer stays as pack. A new Sea ice cover reading shows it, and the ice edge on every layer is its edge.
  • Ice drift now moves the pack with the wind and the ocean currents together, turned to the right of the wind in the north and to the left in the south, as polar explorers measured it.
  • Icebergs calve where ice sheets and glaciers meet the sea, drift on the currents and melt as the water warms. They show with the storms in the Weather year, on continent and region maps too, and sea journeys now count the icebergs met along the way.
  • Plankton blooms: a new Weather year reading shows where the sea turns green in each season, fed by winter mixing, upwelling coasts and river mouths and grown by the returning sun, so temperate seas bloom in spring and the warm open ocean stays clear. A second reading shows where a bloom could turn toxic, and the year's notable blooms appear in its log and on the map.
  • Every world now has an impact history: how many craters its sky has left, how big and how old, thinned by its air and worn by its rain and erosion. Sky & planet shows them as a map layer of fresh, worn and eroded craters, and World facts lists the largest with their age and state.
  • The Sculptor's Crater tool can stamp a crater from that history: choose one from the list and it is placed as an impact crater of its size where it struck.
  • Rain shadows run deeper behind rugged mountain ranges: air coming down into the lee now falls from the range's ridges and not from its average height, so the dry country behind high, broken ranges is drier and the desert edge sits closer to the mountains. Plains, valleys and the windward slopes rain as they did.
  • On a disc world every World tool now works on the disc itself. Landmarks and Deposits are drawn on the disc's map, and clicking one opens its card, which says how far from the hub it is and on what bearing. The Erosion Lab lays its overlays on the disc. The Coast Lab's depth view is the disc itself, Edge included, and a cross-section says whether it runs toward the hub, toward the rim or round the disc. The Climate Lab reads the seasons and compares climates over the disc alone, with its maps drawn as the disc. Tectonics shows the plate map as the disc, and its Show on map button opens the disc's own Tectonic plates view. While a disc is open, Landmarks leaves towns, roads and borders to the disc map's own switches.
  • Landmarks, Deposits and the Erosion Lab's overlays are now also drawn on a continent or region map opened over a globe world, instead of only on the World map hidden behind it.
  • The Planet Advisor talks about a disc as a disc. It asks whether the climate fits each place's distance from the hub, and it gives every finding and every place where people thrive as a distance from the hub and a bearing. A finding that lies past the rim says so, and has no Show me button.
  • The sky explorer's date on a disc now names the season at the hub ("Spring at the hub"). Under a sun that stands over the hub it names no season.
  • Mountains facing the stormy westerlies get noticeably wetter windward slopes: the storms that blow in off the sea now drop much more of their rain as they are forced up the mountains. The land behind those ranges stays as dry as before, so rain shadows are sharper. The steep subtropical sea cliffs that used to get far more rain than anywhere on Earth now top out near Earth's wettest places.

Speed and memory

  • After renaming a place, changing realms or editing the story, the World map reopens almost at once instead of redrawing every river (about 3.5 seconds saved on a Quick world, more on Full), and renaming a place no longer works out the world's mineral deposits two or three times over.
  • Rivers are drawn about a third faster, so building a world takes a little less time and opening its World map, large maps and vector sheets in a new session is quicker; every river and map comes out exactly as before.

Known limits

  • On a disc world, a mountain range that crosses the hub runs straight through it, and a few lakes and salt flats right at the hub draw as narrow wedges pointing at the centre.
  • Deep Time still knows nothing of a giant host: it runs before a world has one.
  • Sharpen as I zoom works on the Stretched projection and on the Physical relief map; on other projections the map says so and offers to stretch it, and other maps stay at the world's own detail. Two neighbouring sharpened windows are separate re-simulations, so small details can shift when a long pan moves to the next one.
  • A Create candidate card can still show a thin land bridge or strait that the built world draws differently: the finished world solves its terrain at its own detail, and keeping the card's narrow joins would mean overriding that terrain. Compare the candidates at the detail you will build to check before choosing.
  • The Erosion Lab's bench, the Coast Lab's counts and Tectonics' plate sizes on a disc are read from the coarse globe the disc is cut from, not from the disc's finer map.
