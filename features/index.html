Field guide

Everything CartoVox does, including the parts nobody finds.

Most of the application lives behind a menu, a rail button or a click on the map. This page walks every workspace in the order the causal chain produces them and calls out the controls that testers keep telling us they never knew were there. The same material ships inside the app under Help, offline.

Current as of the 0.9.0 beta. Version-by-version changes are on the release notes page.

Looking for how to use it?

This page is the inventory: what exists, workspace by workspace. The handbook is the how-to — a first world step by step, the exact path to every control, every export and keyboard key, and a searchable index of where things live. Both pages are current to 0.9; this one says what exists, that one says how to reach it.

New in 0.9

The sheet on the desk.

0.9 is everything implemented after 0.8.3, and it outgrew the number it was planned under. The whole interface has been restated, the map became something you draw on and describe rather than only look at, and every name and every description in a world can now be written on one sheet. The short list, before the workspace-by-workspace walk below; the full account is in the release notes.

  • The interface is a survey sheet — a thumb-index rail, a title block, each workspace a sheet on a desk, floating map cards as dark instruments with a brass top edge, chips as stamps, and every measured number set as a tabular figure. Archivo and DM Mono ship under the OFL beside Newsreader; 205 corner radii were flattened to one geometry, and the light theme was rebuilt as a daylight desk rather than an inverted dark one.
  • The World workspace is map-first — the rendered map is the page, a World / Maps pill sits at the top left above the drawer it opens, and everything that acts on the map lives in the dock along the bottom. The map, below.
  • A Sketch tier — a whole world at quarter resolution in about half a minute, promotable to Quick or Full later as its own project. Preflight and finish.
  • Draw a shape, and say what belongs there — Describe a region places what it can (a named region, a codex page, a gazetteer entry, carrying your words as its lore), moves the ground where the words describe ground, and reports the words it could not place. Sculpt.
  • Turn world — a map tool that moves the meridian the sheet is cut at, so a continent split across the edges can be brought to the middle. The dock.
  • Rename everything, and describe everything — every named thing on one editable sheet, and the prose the generator deliberately leaves blank in one place beside it. Codex.
  • Your map's own rivers — a river drawn on an imported map is read as a river rather than a sea inlet, and you choose whether the world keeps your lines, solves its own, or treats the blue as water. Import a map.
  • Deciding how much of a planet your map is — say what share of the planet your continent covers and CartoVox sizes the sheet to match, with a control that trims the ocean you painted around your coast.
  • The oblique camera — tilt a finished render until the terrain has a far side, with the scale bar and compass left flat on the paper and the picture saying that its vertical scale is invented. Render Studio.
  • The atlas, bound as a book you can print — title page, contents, a page per plate at a real paper size, a colophon carrying the seed, and every plate embedded at its own resolution. Exports.
  • Settlements you can play on — a town leaves as a Universal VTT (.dd2vtt) or a Foundry scene, with walls, doors and line of sight decided rather than guessed. Settlement Studio.
  • Encounter maps with height — give a wall or a tower a height in your scene's own units and it casts a shadow from one sun, with nothing tilted and every footprint still on its square. Scenes.
  • Watch the world being made — a build shows the land and relief about seven seconds in, then the climate and biomes, drawn from fields the simulation had already computed. A watched build and an unwatched one produce identical saved fields.
  • An illustrated first-run tour, and an example world — six panels of drawings instead of 1,587 words, and one click builds Eldrebrenn: a complete project in your own library, measured at 83.9 seconds and 179 MB. Library.
  • Exports that carry what they claimed to — real GeoJSON coastlines with lakes as holes (agreeing with the saved land mask to 99.94% of cells), and OBJ/STL meshes at the whole grid instead of 3.2% of it.
  • No network request of its own — the daily update check is gone, along with the installer download it fed and the certificate bundle it needed. New builds are announced on the Discord server, and App settings → Updates & support says so.

Orientation

The desk

One window, five rooms, one record. The left rail carries Search, Create, World, Write and Library; each workspace has its own contextual tabs across the top; the bottom of the rail holds Layout, Settings and Help. Since 0.9 each workspace is a sheet on that desk, and says which sheet it is in its own bottom corner.

  • Search — a command palette and a library-wide index in one. Type a world, realm, settlement, natural region, lore entry, Chronicle era, campaign or aquifer and jump to it; type an action (“Describe an empire”, “Define a tongue”, “Write what the world is about”) and it opens the panel. Matching is whole-word and prefix, never a guess.
  • Each workspace is one sheet — Create is a strip of actions above a folding brief; World is map-first, with a two-segment pill above its drawer and a command dock along the bottom; Write is one strip above the page with More and Book menus and a single panel switch.
  • Workspaces resume — World reopens on the tab you last used (Map, Atlas, Travel, Campaign or Scenes); so does Write. Settings can turn the memory off without erasing it.
  • Layout — a drawer that hides regions of the interface: the top bar, rails, panels. Choices persist; a fixed handle always leads back. Focus map strips every piece of chrome and restores it exactly on exit.
  • The generation dock — a long build keeps running while you work elsewhere; the dock shows phase, plate, ETA and queued jobs, and survives navigation and reload.
  • Help — the manual, known limits, the licence, third-party notices and a task-based capability tour that points at climate investigation, tectonic direction, import, sculpting, travel, writing, campaigns, publication and scientific export.
Easy to miss
  • ⌘, (or Ctrl,) opens App settings from anywhere.
  • The rail's Search is also where the command palette lives — it takes verbs as well as names.
  • Hide the top bar and the Layout handle still offers a way back to Settings.

Create

Four ways in, then a purpose.

Create opens with a choice, not a form: Generate a new world, Import my existing map, Draw my own world or Build through deep time. A second choice — campaigning, climate study, an illustrated atlas, a base for external artwork, or writing and worldbuilding — tunes visible options rather than hiding a preset system. Skip — I know what I want goes straight to the sheet.

  • One strip above a folding brief. Since 0.9 the doors are in the stage rather than in a panel of their own, and the strip carries the route (Generate / Import/Draw, with Why this route and Change), the setup counter, Advanced settings, Preview this seed and the gold button. Below it sit the world shape, the seed, Reset all, and two grey rows: OUTPUT (what you will get) and COST (how long, how much disk).
  • Foundation map — a row that names the imported or drawn map your next world will be built on, beside the button that uses it, with Remove map in the same row. An attached map replaces the geography of everything generated from that sheet until you take it off.
  • Setup guide — an optional six-step guide (choose how to begin, write the brief, name the peoples and their tongues, preview a seed, find strong matches, review the cost) that shows which simulation systems each setting affects. The strip counts it as Setup · n/6; Expert view puts it away.
  • Reset all restores the entire creation surface, not a hidden subset. Draft recovery brings back unsaved Advanced-settings work after an accidental Back.
  • Create Sketch world vs Create Quick world vs Create full-resolution world vs Generate publication atlas — the gold button says which cost you are committing to.

The World Brief

Five numbered folds, each summarising itself in its own header so a closed brief still says what it holds: 1 Your story, 2 Planet & geography, 3 Peoples & languages, 4 World constraints, 5 Tectonic intent.

  • World thesis — a paragraph about what the world is, what is scarce or contested, and what the map must show. The reading finds landforms, climates and ways of life it names and checks them against the structured brief, one Apply per cue and Apply all for the lot. Which words does it know? prints its own vocabulary, and a worked example thesis exists for every silhouette.
  • Must have / Prefer / Avoid — hard constraints and soft ones. A standalone continent count counts connected landmasses of at least 2 million km²; matches come before alternatives, misses state the actual count, and the saved world re-checks the final simulation.
  • Story frame — epic world history, and other frames that shape the generated lore's emphasis.
  • Geography — nine continental starting points: Balanced Earth-like, Pangaea / Supercontinent, Shattered Archipelago, Inland Sea / Mediterranean, Twin Hemispheres, Rifted Continent / Great Rift, Drowned Interior / Flooded Lowlands, Fjordland / Long Coasts, Equatorial Belt. Your saved recipes appear in the same list.
  • Planet type is chosen separately from that geography, so changing the kind of planet does not have to masquerade as changing its landmasses: Earth-like, Ice age, Lush and warm, Tilted world, The long day, The long winter, Great moon, Young and violent, Greenhouse ocean, Thin air & cold desert, Dim star. Nineteen presets in all, since Balanced Earth-like is both.
  • Star, orbit & moon folds into the same section, summarising itself as the day length, the year, the stellar flux and the lunar tide it currently describes.
  • “Lush” means few deserts — it weakens subtropical subsidence and strengthens inland moisture recycling instead of merely raising rainfall. “Few deserts” in a thesis and “Avoid: deserts” pull the same lever.

Nations, cultures and tongues — and the Tectonic Director

Two panels most people never open, because they sit below the brief. This is where an author's empires go.

  • Nation Brief — name realms and capitals before generation; request homeland traits, biome preferences, resources, trade goods and cultures; drop a homeland pin on a map with a visible search area and plain-language size choices (exact latitude, longitude and distance stay optional). Accepted homelands enter the civilisation pipeline as founding hearths; locked names flow through the naming registry so maps, dossiers and lore agree.
  • Tongues — define a language by blending ten real-language-inspired flavours (French, Russian, Latin, Old English, Spanish, Cherokee, Greek, Old Norse, Arabic, Japanese) by weight, describe it in a sentence and watch the chips follow, add words you like, and preview a dozen names with glosses and pronunciation before the world exists. A tongue travels with the brief and can be preferred or locked to a realm.
  • Tectonic Director — an interactive coordinate map for craton nuclei, plate seeds, requested convergent, divergent and transform margins, drift bearings and speeds, locks and mantle hotspots. Partial intent is fine; CartoVox fills the rest. Preflight flags contradictory requests before a long build, and the finished world reports which intentions were satisfied, compromised or impossible.
  • Domestication — whether wild-crop packages may relocate civilisation hearths is an explicit choice on the route, not a hidden setting.

Seed search

  • Preview this seed draws one candidate; Find 6 strong matches scores reproducible seeds against every measurable requirement. Search depth sets how many it tries — Quick 18 seeds, Thorough 36 (the default), Deep 72 — and nothing is saved by a search.
  • Candidate cards — Preview, Compare and More in one row; the consequential creation button on its own row. Cards distinguish measured checks from constraints that still need author review, and previews are labelled approximate because the production grid can change small islands.
  • Show 24 controlled variations — structurally distinct alternatives of the same brief. Shortlist filters narrow the set.
  • Favourite, reject, annotate — favourites and rejections become an adjustable taste signal for later searches; notes and rejection reasons never leave the browser. Hard constraints stay authoritative.
  • Compare lays candidates side by side with a coast-complexity scale and the same measured/review badges.
  • Results stay between visits; a run's settings are preserved even when it fails or is cancelled, and the completion panel offers Adjust these settings.

Advanced settings, presets and recipes

The sheet has 306 inputs in twenty groups, in the order the simulation uses them, each with a note and its allowed range. Nothing is hidden in a module.

  • Planet — radius, gravity, rotation and day length, orbital year, axial tilt, eccentricity and periapsis phase, star luminosity and distance, land share, tides, atmospheric pressure, an explicit greenhouse forcing, ocean salinity, and one dominant moon's mass, distance, period and inclination. Authored year lengths are allowed; a Kepler mismatch is shown, not corrected.
  • Ice is emergent by default; ask for a global cover from 0 to 0.98 and the dossier records the temperature adjustment it took.
  • Seasons — climate at 4, 8 or 12 samples per orbital year, weighted by orbital residence time so eccentric worlds are not over-counted at perihelion.
  • Tectonics, relief, hydrology, lakes, glaciation, erosion, shelves, coasts, hearths — every lever has a plain-language row: more islands, higher mountains, more perennial rivers, more closed saline lakes, an ice age, a greenhouse world. Settings are bounded only where a value would break the simulation; a 62° tilt with tropical poles is still reachable.
  • Recipes — Save as recipe keeps the preset plus only the settings you changed (up to 24, 64 KB per import). Export recipe / Import from file moves one between machines; imports preview and ask before applying. Last generated is written automatically at the start of every run in a reserved slot, so the settings behind a disappointing world are never lost.
  • Terrain image interpretation, magic source and cost, naming-family rules, apostrophe policy, atlas label density, lake detail and map palette are dropdowns with friendly labels over stable internal values.
  • Changing the World shape asks “Discard 3 changed settings?” first; Clear overrides says how many it will lose. The whole sheet survives a reload, and the Advanced settings button on Create carries the override count.

Simulation detail, preflight, finish, plates and lettering

  • Three tiers, not two. Sketch is a quarter of Quick's resolution — a 360 × 180 grid with reduced moisture and current budgets, measured at 30.5 seconds with an illustrated atlas on a fast Apple Silicon Mac. Quick is half resolution; Full is the production grid every published map is drawn from. Every tier runs the same simulation and saves the same archive members, so the codex, dossier, gazetteer, data maps and exports all work on a Sketch — the difference is how many cells the simulation was evaluated on. The same three-way choice is in Create and in Settings → Creation defaults → First build, and the two stay in step.
  • A Sketch can be promoted one rung at a time. The saved-world rebuild takes a target tier, so a Sketch can become Quick without paying for Full. As before, the rebuild starts a separate project and leaves the world you chose exactly as it was. An unrecognised tier is refused with a message naming the three that exist, rather than quietly downgraded.
  • Preflight reports the expected output range and free disk for the chosen grid, finish and sheet set, keeps a 1 GiB reserve, and learns from completed builds of the same shape on this machine. Reference bands: 45–100 MiB Quick, 180–360 MiB Full without plates, 337–883 MiB with every publication plate.
  • A build shows you the world it is making. Land and relief appear about seven seconds into a Quick build, and the map returns wearing its climate and biomes once rainfall and temperature have decided what grows where. The pictures come from fields the simulation had already computed, add a fraction of a second, and change nothing: a watched build and an unwatched one produce identical saved fields. A frame that cannot be drawn is skipped in silence.
  • Atlas finish — Draft or Publication. Sheet groups — world, continent and regional sheets, selectable. Village label density for the minor names.
  • Atlas lettering — Labeled only, No labels, or Both from the same illustrated base; the choice applies to world, continent and regional sheets, and the missing variant can be added later without re-simulating.
  • Stop after this plate finishes the active image cleanly and stores the rest; Render remaining plates in World → Atlas draws them later from saved data.
  • Best-quality drawings rebuilds a Quick world's eligible maps at 4× drawing density from the exact saved simulation; Rebuild at full simulation resolution makes a separate project from the same seed and recipe.
  • Progress weights phases by measured wall time — naming gets its real share — and recalibrates the ETA from completed plate timings. Interrupted runs are detected and can be resumed from their stage checkpoint or removed.

Import a map

  • Formats — PNG, JPEG, TIFF, numeric height grids, GeoJSON land outlines, shapefiles, GeoTIFFs, Azgaar exports, and OBJ or STL terrain surfaces (X east, Y north, Z metres). A GeoTIFF or an Azgaar export is read as what it is rather than as an anonymous image, and land can be raised from a shapefile. Limits scale with free disk and are reported before the import.
  • The coastline reader distinguishes pale oceans, dark silhouettes with lakes, sepia maps, painted biome maps and true heightfields; painted-map imports learn blue water shades including bathymetric bands, keep continents that touch the edge, and detect a rectangular map inside a dark interface border (Use detected map area). Map lettering that reads as land is cleaned out; round islets are left alone.
  • It learns the sea from the sea, not from the frame. On a whole-world sheet the colours crowding the border are the ice caps, so a planet a quarter covered in continents used to come back as 80% land; the water is now learned from the blue that reaches an edge of the sheet, wherever it sits. A tinted country drawn out to the coast stays on the map, an island painted close to the colour of the sea keeps its coast from about 12° of hue away instead of 40°, and a landlocked sheet — a kingdom with a lake, a river basin — is no longer read inside out. The panel says which way it read your sheet, and Water tolerance moves the line between shallows and sea.
  • An imported map's plates are fitted to the coastline it brought. Plate boundaries used to cut across the land you supplied; measured against a real import, boundary-on-land fell from 40% to 4.1%. Imported terrain also stops reading as ice: heights were being scaled so that far too much of the world sat above 2,000 m, and a round-trip of a saved world's own elevation now lands where it started.
  • Zoom & inspect opens the upload and the interpreted coastline in a full-window viewer. Paint land / Paint water, Protect region (frozen in gold, with its one-pixel rim), Undo correction and Reset corrections correct the reading and survive another detection pass.
  • Trace the coastline yourself — click corners or sketch freehand over the faded upload; Magnetic tracing follows nearby image edges between anchors; Auto trace offers Fine, Balanced or Smooth outlines and a minimum island area, and reports what it removed. Tracing saves recoverable drafts automatically; Resume tracing reopens them after a restart, and Save draft file writes a portable .cartovox-trace.json with the reference image embedded.
  • The questions the map leaves open — where borrowed relief should gather, how high the peaks reach, whether the land is young or worn, how deep the seas are, how wet and warm the world is, how closely climate should follow painted colours. Every answer is an ordinary Advanced setting; an unanswered question keeps the preset.
  • The rivers on this map — narrow blue inside your coastline is read as a river and drawn back over your map in blue with a count, because a lake or a sea is wide and a river is narrow wherever you cut across it. Keep the rivers I drew cuts each one into the surface as a strictly descending channel, so the ground the hydrology solves has one lowest path and it is your line; on a hand-drawn test map 9 of 9 rivers were followed, 89% of their drawn cells, with a mean cut of 96 m. Let CartoVox put the rivers takes your lines out of the coastline just the same, so your continent is whole, then leaves where the water runs to the terrain, rainfall and slope — and the same map came out with its own rivers falling on 8 of your 9 lines anyway. They are not rivers keeps the blue as water, right for straits and fjords, and is the only one of the three that leaves an inlet cut into your land. A river may cut a valley through a mountain but may not delete it: both the channel and the ground beside it are bounded at 400 m.
  • Your land on the planet — type the share of the planet your land should cover and CartoVox resizes the sheet to reach it, measured on the sphere so ground near the poles does not count for more than it is. The panel names the full range your map can reach, says so plainly when you ask for one it cannot, and stops where the poles do rather than scaling a taller-than-2:1 sheet quietly back off.
  • Ocean kept around your land — an imported sheet is usually a continent adrift in its author's painted sea, and the width control used to resize both. Trim from the whole sheet down to a crop tight at the coastline: a test map that is 85% water went from 11.5% of the planet to 34.7% without a pixel of the coast moving. The coastline itself is never touched; CartoVox fills what it removes.
  • Map review or World settings — the two share a column, with a switch above them. Advanced settings opens the settings sheet; a map arriving opens its review, because a coastline wants checking first. Either is one click from the other, for an uploaded map and a drawn one alike.
  • What a map does not say is simulated: climate, rivers, biomes, settlements, names and lore. Provenance records that the coastline was imported or traced, over which file, and which of the three river answers the world was built with.

Draw your world

  • Brushes — Land, Water, Islands, Outline, Raise, Lower, Smooth, Flatten, with size, strength and hard-edge controls, undo and redo, and a seeded starting shape to sculpt. The ocean wraps east–west, because it is a planet.
  • Rivers are carved as descending valleys so the hydrology solver has a lowest path; the finished world reports how much of each drawn river it actually followed. Beside the river tool is the same choice the importer offers — keep the ones I draw or let CartoVox put them — with no third answer, because a stroke drawn with the river tool is a river. It changes the ground and not just the export: the canvas carves a valley along each drawn river, handing them over stops it, and switching back and forth returns exactly the ground you started with.
  • Labels (place, region, sea, mountains, river), kingdom borders, cities, land cover, roads and landmarks return as an author-canon layer on the finished map; drawn borders and cities are honoured by the build and reported when they cannot be.
  • Reference image — drop a map and the coastline is previewed automatically (colour map, line drawing, light/dark mask or transparency readings); Mark land / Mark water and Detect coastline correct it, Apply coastline is one undoable change. Magnetic outlines follow the picture's edges.
  • Save drawing as PNG heightmap, GeoJSON, STL or OBJ, and reopen it later; Use this drawing hands it to the same coastline review as an import.

Deep Time

  • Paint an initial supercontinent (or let one be built), run hundreds of millions of years of breakup, spreading, subduction, collision, suturing and erosion, then scrub every saved era with play, pause, four speeds, looping and a narration of each era's dominant events.
  • Look ahead keeps the run going past the era the world is built from — 100, 200 or 400 million years — so the timeline shows where its plates go next. Keep a future era instead and the world is built there.
  • Promote an era and the finished world inherits its plate inventory, welded groups, terranes, crust age and thickness, rift progress and relief — then runs the full climate, rivers, biomes, settlements, names and lore on top.
  • Milankovitch forcing stores each era's tilt, eccentricity and precession; the promoted era carries that exact orbital state. Ice, isostasy & sea level (v1) is an opt-in feedback model that saves the era's elevation relative to its own sea level, so the world inherits an ice-age coastline.
  • An import and a Deep Time era cannot be combined: they are competing foundations, and the app says so.

Reseed, replicate, build another like this

  • Reseed this world (World tab, under How this world was built) builds a new world beside the current one from the same inputs under another seed, in three scopes: everything the seed decides, the story only (places of power, names, realms and lore on identical land) or the land only. Imported and drawn foundations travel across; lineage.json records where the new world came from.
  • Build another like this loads a world's preset and settings into Create without the seed. Replicate a saved world with one click from its recipe.
  • Every world writes a world recipe beside itself: seed, preset, every changed setting, the brief and thesis, nations, tectonic intent, output options and the imported or drawn foundation. One Save, one Export, one Import for the whole sheet.

World → Map

The map is the workspace.

World is map-first: the rendered map is the page. A two-segment pill at the top left — World, Maps — opens the drawer beneath it, and everything that acts on the map lives in the dock along the bottom: View, Export, Tools, the zoom controls, Focus map and fullscreen. The Planet card floats at the top right and can be put away; the colour key and the coordinate line sit bottom left. Everything else is a mode that comes to the front when you ask for it.

Fifty-four views, one geography

The Maps drawer groups every generated view — fifty-four standing sheets on a current world, plus one atlas plate per world, continent, region and city, and the projection products. Each has a reading key, a plain-language source method — Simulation output, Calculated from simulation, Planning estimate, Rule-based interpretation, Visual presentation, Author-edited layer or What-if preview — its projection, working scale, limitations and a downloadable Provenance JSON. Views whose saved fields an older world lacks are marked unavailable rather than invented.

Easy to miss
  • Show classes in the map key filters categories on soils, bedrock, resources, realms, biomes and hydrology; All, None and More/Less live there. Filters survive a change of drawing density and update the globe and the PNG download.
  • The numbered agricultural cradles on the Domestication map are real controls: select one for its founder crops, package strength, ancestry and settlement influence.
  • Every generated data view has a Why here? reading on click, and the Map source card's Provenance download says what kind of evidence you are looking at.

The cell inspector

  • Click any cell: elevation, plate and boundary type, climate, seasonal runoff and discharge, snowpack, groundwater, permafrost, watershed and depression IDs, stream order and channel form, lake depth and outlet status, biome, realm, settlement suitability and fantasy intensity. Metric or imperial units.
  • Explain one climate cell — why it is warmer, colder, wetter or drier than latitude predicts: elevation, continentality, upwind terrain, moisture supply and the saved circulation, with the upwind moisture path traced on the map.
  • Read the dossier for the settlement, realm, natural region or biome under the click, straight from the gazetteer.
  • Rename the feature at the clicked cell, move or remove a settlement, or place a named settlement on any land with at least 20% saved suitability. Canonical names propagate to maps, dossiers, lore, routes, tongues and exports.
  • The hover reading, the reading key and the coordinates stack without covering each other; the inspector retries with fresh data if a revision changes underneath it.

The pill, the dock, and the View and Tools menus

Since 0.9 there is no toolbar above the map. The pill at the top left holds the two ambient modes, World and Maps; the dock along the bottom holds View, Export and Tools, then zoom out, 1:1, zoom in, Focus map and fullscreen, with the resolution line above it. Nothing in any of them ever re-simulates.

View — Map appearance

  • Map colors — Natural (sage, sandstone, deep teal), Verdant, Parchment, Dusk, Tropical, Ember, Aurora, Illustrated (sapphire deeps, cyan shallows) and Classic. Each world remembers its own; live class swatches, continuous scales, world cards, globe textures and new Render Studio compositions follow.
  • Projection — Stretched (the simulation's own equirectangular sheet) to begin with, then Equal Earth, Robinson, Mollweide, Mercator, and Two hemispheres: the Gott double disk sharing the equator as its rim, mirrored so coastlines meet when printed back to back. Overlays, hover readings and measurements land on the same spot in every projection, and since 0.9 choosing a projection no longer turns the world: the meridian a sheet opens at resets with the zoom and the pan each time a sheet is shown.
  • Detail — Auto, Original, 2×, 3×, 4×. Continuous fields interpolate bicubically, categorical ones stay nearest-neighbour, river reaches redraw as vector lines weighted by discharge. The persistent badge states the simulated cell size, always.
  • Planet — show or hide the globe card. Map aids — Minimap, Split view, Render, Time strip, HD region and Grid, which are presentation rather than instruments and so live here.

Tools

  • Measure — great-circle distance on the saved planetary radius, correct across the antimeridian and near the poles, with per-leg distances; Finish path (or Enter) locks a route without closing a shape.
  • Landmarks — overlay capitals, settlements, ports, places of power, author story pins and mountain passes; select a marker for its lore, realm and culture context. Filters are remembered locally.
  • Explain climate — pick a cell and read why it is warmer, colder, wetter or drier than latitude predicts, with the upwind moisture path drawn.
  • Turn world 0.9 — drag sideways to move the meridian the sheet is cut at, on and on in both directions, so a continent split across the left and right edges can be brought to the middle. It is a mode you enter, because a drag that quietly reprojected every marker, route and graticule made ordinary panning feel like the map was fighting back; off, a drag pans and a scroll zooms. An equirectangular sheet turns without asking the server at all, and fifteen full turns land exactly back where they started; shaped sheets — Equal Earth, Robinson, Mollweide — are redrawn once, when you let go. Markers, routes, the graticule, the hover reading and cell inspection all ask one module where a degree lands, so they turn with it. Switching the tool off keeps the orientation you chose; 1:1 is the way home. Only the sheets that can carry a turn are offered it — an atlas plate, a cartography style and a finished projection product each carry a frame, a border and lettering laid out for the meridian they open at — and on anything else the tool is greyed out and says why.

Labs and the Regional lens

  • Climate Lab — seasonal temperature, pressure, wind and rainfall frames to scrub; moisture-path inspection; located plausibility checks; a bounded what-if that estimates how selected climate controls would change the saved world, clearly labelled an approximation; and a review bundle (seed, resolved configuration, model response, findings) written locally for a specialist.
  • Weather year — the saved seasonal climate as an animated year with a selectable frame count; a radar view follows precipitation and seeded storm systems; storm frequency, intensity and variability under a reproducible seed. Save it as a named weather sheet that Travel and the manuscript share.
  • Erosion Lab — the same stream-power, hillslope-diffusion and floodplain-deposition kernel as generation, on a non-destructive coarse bench: before/after relief and drainage, what moved, mass balance. The world changes only on an explicit accept-and-re-simulate.

Regional downscaling refines a window at 2×, 4× or 8× with fine terrain, lapse-rate temperature, windward rain and re-solved drainage, labelled separately from the coarse truth.

The Planet card and the 3D terrain explorer

  • Planet — a live globe in the corner with spin, a link to the map's centre, a light/dark printable background, and a switch between a sphere and two Gott disks you can drag around their shared axis. It hides independently of everything else. Its compass reports the place you are looking at rather than a heading: the globe's up vector is the planet's own axis, so north really is up, and the indicator is spent on the latitude and longitude beneath the centre of the view — “over the north pole” when you are straight down the axis.
  • 3D terrain (Tools in the dock) — three views from one saved sample: a rectangular Landscape, a displaced-sphere Planet you can orbit into either hemisphere, and Voxels in 100, 250, 500 or 1,000 m terraces. Detail presets from 96 to 2,048 samples on the longest edge; natural colours or biome, elevation, realm and climate palettes; vertical relief, smoothing, seabed relief, water opacity, rivers and lakes; sun direction and lighting presets; perspective, orthographic, top and low-angle cameras; an inspector that always reports saved cell values; PNG export of any view. Select a region on the planet to open it as a landscape. Rendering is local, in a worker, and never exceeds the saved simulation grid.

Sculpt, Select feature, revisions

  • Sculpt — Peak, Ridge, Plateau, Island, Strait, River valley, Basin, Deep water, Coast, Flatten, Smooth, Detail, Protect area, Preserve terrain, Settlement, Story pin and Describe a region. The brush previews its footprint, falloff, bearing, radius, cumulative elevation delta, affected area, raised and lowered cells and provisional coast crossings on the map before anything is applied; several operations stack with undo and redo.
  • Sculpting with your hands 0.9 — a click places the brush exactly as the panel describes it; holding the pointer and dragging states the footprint in kilometres on the map itself, and releasing hands the pointer the height, with both numbers read out beside the cursor as they change. A painted stroke is a row of ordinary nudges spaced on the ground rather than on the screen, so the same gesture means the same thing at any zoom. Ridge and River valley are drawn as trails: click each peak or bend in turn, hold any one of them to give that summit its own height, and finish on the last point — the whole crest is one edit, previewed, undone, listed and re-simulated as a single record.
  • Describe a region 0.9 — draw a circle or a square on the map and say what belongs there. A description naming something CartoVox can place gets it placed: a named region on the map, a page in the codex, an entry in the gazetteer, carrying your own words as its lore. Where the words describe ground rather than a landmark — volcanic, highlands, sunken — the ground moves, through the same sculpt records a hand edit would have written. Words it read and could not place are reported back rather than silently dropped.
  • The places you put on the map stay put — cities and borders you draw hold their positions against the world's own, instead of being displaced by the generator's next pass.
  • Select feature reads the editable terrain, water, ecology, settlement, route, realm and magical-region features under a point. Remove or reshape land, islands, lakes, rivers, biome regions and mountain terrain; move or remove settlements; reshape routes and realm borders; edit magical regions. An operation that cannot honestly preserve a generated feature says so and stages the closest supported change.
  • Re-simulate states which downstream systems will be rebuilt before it runs, uses the world's recorded seed, can be cancelled while it is still safe, renders new plates in staging and swaps them only when they exist. Every apply creates a restorable revision under revisions/r####/; author canon, locked names and lore overrides survive it.
  • Checkpoints can be named; continuity diffing after a sculpt reports what moved: planet stats, landmass areas, hearth movement in km, realm territory shares and any name that changed.
  • A world being rebuilt no longer looks finished. Applying terrain edits marks the project Rebuilding edits in the Library while the work is in flight, so a force-quit at hour two cannot leave an entry offering every action over a world whose maps and archive no longer agree. A failure before the commit restores the project; a failure after it reports the project as incomplete; a force-quit is reconciled on the next start.

Render Studio

  • Nine recipes — Physical atlas, Pastel kingdoms, Banded heightmap, Biome atlas, Antique parchment, Survey blueprint, Print outline, Pure landmass and Illustrated realm. Save named recipes on this device or share them as small JSON files with their stamps embedded.

Reorder them; set opacity, colour, line width, size and label halos; choose grayscale or sepia; keep alpha in transparent exports.
  • Illustrated realm paints watercolour land cover with snow above each biome's snowline; places original hand-drawn mountains, hills, volcanoes with glowing craters, conifer, broadleaf and tropical trees, dead trees on ash and blight, reeds in wetlands; cracks ice into floes and salt pans, ripples dunes, streaks grassland, shows reef shallows. Glyphs are placed from the world's own seed so a 1K preview carries the same forests as the 16K export.
  • Titles and captions — positioned text that can rotate, curve up or down, use its own typeface and tracking, or follow a copied drawing path; click the preview to place a caption, compass or scale bar. Place-label editing — search a saved name, change its display text, typography or size, hide it, or nudge it, without touching the canonical name or the settlement's position.
  • Stamps and shapes — a decoration canvas with twelve original vector symbols, imported PNG/JPEG/WebP stamps organised into shareable art packs, freeform paths, polygons, Bézier curves with editable handles, brush strokes and soft dabs with Normal, Multiply or Screen blending, eraser objects and paintable masks, textures and shadows, groups, snapping, canvas zoom, 40 steps of undo, and full keyboard control. Up to 200 objects per recipe.
  • Scene paper — editable keep-interior, woodland-encounter and village-square layouts with square or hex encounter grids, colours, spacing and author units, on paper that keeps its drawing scale.
  • Oblique view 0.9 — beside every finished render that can carry it. Every sheet CartoVox draws is a plan view; press this and the sheet tilts until the terrain has a far side, so ridges stand up, valleys go behind them and the coast has a horizon. It tilts the finished sheet rather than each layer, so nothing comes apart: a river is still in its valley and a name is still on its town, because they are the same pixels, carried with the ground they name the way a hand-drawn panorama does it. How tall the mountains stand is asked for as a share of the picture rather than as a multiplier, the sides of the terrain are shaded so you can see which pixels are the map and which are the camera's, and the scale bar, compass and graticule stay flat on the paper — a scale bar bent over a hill would promise a measurement the picture cannot keep. The picture says so on screen and inside the PNG: horizontal distances are the map's, vertical ones are not to any scale. Your render is untouched; the oblique is a separate picture beside it.
  • Bundles include captions.svg and objects.svg beside the PNG layers, so lettering and artwork stay editable; render manifests record the appearance and palette for reproducibility. Relief depth — cast shadows and sky openness — changes 0.5% of land on a whole-world sheet and 22.5% at regional scale, which is where an escarpment finally throws a shadow across the ground below it.

World → Atlas

The illustrated atlas

Painted base, inked coastlines, terrain built from stamped symbols — drawn from the saved fields, so terrain, climate, rivers, settlements, names and lore are never re-run for a sheet.

  • Three scales, three languages — a world sheet (>12 km/px) paints biomes, inks the coast, draws great rivers and city dots; a continent sheet (4–12 km/px) adds ridge-aligned peaks, forest masses with decorated edges, realm tints and named rivers; a regional sheet (<4 km/px) uses the full stamp language, interior forest, third-tier towns, ports and the keeps that hold the passes.
  • Nothing invents geography — peaks follow the ridgeline with snow above the local snowline, trees follow canopy and switch species by biome, dunes follow the ergs, volcanoes sit on real arcs, rivers carry width from real discharge, and deltas appear only where the hydrology reports one.
  • Coast and sea — a crisp inked shore, a pale halo, concentric hachures, the shelf break, engraved isobaths and a faint portolan rhumb web. Gulfs, bays, inland seas and straits are found by closing the coastline at 760 km and named by measurement, so a strait is called a strait because it measures like one.
  • Type — four sizes, three treatments: water italic, political tracked caps, physical small caps; river names set glyph by glyph along the channel. A name that cannot satisfy the neat line, its feature mask and the busyness under it is dropped and reported; nothing overlaps.
  • Eight style sheets — Tolkien sepia, Swiss analytical relief, modern atlas, antique nautical, working ink, grimdark, the clean land-and-water base and a political sheet — plus a realm map grown by travel cost and a vector sheet that zooms without blurring. They are drawn beside the plates and appear under Cartography in the Maps drawer.
  • Six reading desks — Royal Surveyor, Bluewater Navigator, Field Naturalist, Court Chronicler, Monastery Copyist, Forbidden Archivist: curated sets of views for a purpose, each listing only what actually exists for this build.
  • Labelled and unlabelled variants are grouped under one sheet name; redraw the lettering after a rename, add the missing variant later, or Create publication atlas for a world built without one. Render remaining plates finishes a stopped run.
  • City plans — a plan per major settlement, drawn from its site, and available to the manuscript's Insert map.
  • The impossible layer — blighted wastes downwind of settled country, ashlands downwind of volcanoes, firelands on young rock, fungal forests in hot wet closed canopy, silverwoods deep in old forest, frostbound reaches, glass wastes on desert floors, floating isles over broken crust, drowned ruins on the shelf, a maelstrom in deep water — each placed by rule with its cause in the dossier, leylines joining them as a spanning tree. Switch it off entirely or drop one kind.
  • Map gallery in the Library shows every plate, style render and city plan from every world on one wall, newest first, filterable by world, kind and lettering, with a lightbox and a jump to the sheet's own Atlas tab. A sheet drawn before a later edit says so.

World → Travel

The Journey Laboratory

  • From / To any named settlement, with suggestions; Swap endpoints. Mode — on foot, horseback, caravan or sailing ship. Season — typical year or a specific one from a saved weather sheet. Objective — Balanced, Fastest, Safer or Trade-minded. Party size. Map beneath the route — any physical view.
  • Land journeys report distance, time, ascent and descent with an elevation profile, realm borders crossed, river crossings, established-road share, dry stretches, food, water, fodder and reserve capacity — and say when a route needs staged caches. Market stops, local goods, toll exposure and expected border delay are named.
  • Ship journeys use nautical analytics instead: wind and current assistance, open-water legs, port intervals, sea hazards, cargo and potable-water capacity.
  • Journey weather follows the trip from its departure day to its wind-adjusted arrival; routes beyond the 400-day planning window still solve and say why forecast detail is unavailable.
  • Alternatives can be selected as the active result; Save journey to campaign keeps it as an authored story track whose gold handles can be dragged on the map.
  • Routes take the cheapest ground — slope, altitude, marsh, river crossings, magical hazards, resource provinces — which is why they hug valleys and find passes, and why realm frontiers fall on ranges and straits.

Settlement Studio

Villages and towns, planned street-first

The generator used to choose an outline and scatter buildings inside it, so every village came out as the same blob of unaligned boxes. It now lays the street network first, takes the blocks those streets enclose, divides each block into plots, and fits a building to each plot facing its street. The settlement's outline is whatever the finished blocks add up to.

The plan comes from the world

  • Eight plans that differ in structure, not in jitter — street village, green, crossroads, radial, organic, harbour, hilltop and twin neighbourhoods. Which one you get is chosen from the approach roads, water and relief the saved world already records.
  • Quarters set their own rules — market, green, craft, townhouse, gentry, manor, temple, dockside, poor quarter and crofts each carry their own plot size, coverage, setback and building forms. Poor quarters deliberately do not line up, and are grown as one district.
  • Every plot faces a street, and every building on one side of a street sits on a shared frontage line, so a row reads as a row.
  • Water and roads load automatically from the saved hydrology: the river's real width, ponds, springs, public wells, the mapped groundwater area and the regional approach directions. Every value stays changeable under an explicit override, and Reset restores what the world says.
  • The countryside scales with population under an editable land budget: connected farms with strip fields and furrows, readable crop types, orchards, hedges, working buildings and biome woodland. The sheet is sized from the settlement, and the hectares beyond it are reported rather than drawn.
  • Bridges are chosen, not sprinkled — roads and walls stop at the water's edge, ordinary roads pass beneath surface water, and river crossings are a spaced set picked by road importance and span. Wall gates appear where roads actually cross.
  • A 40,000-person city generates in about a tenth of the time it used to take.

Artwork that is drawn, not stretched

  • Roofs are built from each building's own footprint, with ridges, hips and courses, so an L-shaped or long narrow house gets a roof that fits it. Previously one of six drawings was squashed to whatever rectangle it was given.
  • Fifty-seven pieces, up from thirty: eleven tree species, windmills, watermills, granaries, forges, chapels, gatehouses, fountains and troughs, beehives, livestock pens, woodpiles, fences, shrines, stocks, boats, jetties, and vineyard, flax and fallow field treatments.
  • Three art directions — Illustrated adventure by default, with engraved and tabletop beside it, matching the application's own.
  • Scenery behaves — orchard trees keep their proportions in irregular plots, crop rows follow field boundaries, woodland forms uneven groves, and useful scenery sits near buildings or fields.

Designing one by hand

  • Steer the generator — street density, plot size, building coverage, irregularity, the share given to poor quarters, and how far the countryside reaches.
  • Choose artwork by looking at it — a picker showing the drawings themselves, filtered to what the chosen feature can actually use, with search and an explicit automatic option.
  • Place features on the canvas and have them turn to face the nearest street, or scatter a copse of trees in one click that undoes as a single step.
  • Draw a quarter on the map and rebuild only that part. The streets and every building outside it stay exactly where they are.
  • Edit anything — move, reshape, style, label, add and delete buildings, trees, fields, roads, water and defences, with undo. Edited geometry stays paired with the SVG, PNG and tile exports.
  • It explains itself — which planner is active, why a control is unavailable, what each colour channel paints, and an unsaved draft you can restore from the first screen.

Settlements you can play on 0.9

  • Universal VTT (.dd2vtt) — walls, doors and the map image in one file, read by Foundry through the Universal Battlemap Importer, by Roll20 through the community importer, and natively by Fantasy Grounds and Arkenforge. The file carries its map image and declares the pixel density that image actually has rather than the one a tabletop would prefer.
  • Foundry scene — the same plan as a scene Foundry opens with no module at all; the image travels beside it.
  • What blocks sight is decided, not guessed — buildings, town walls, towers and rock block line of sight; fences and pens go in the objects list instead, because a waist-high fence stops a cart and not a view; gates become doors that open; roads and fields are not obstacles.
  • Choose your square — five feet, ten feet, one metre or two. A settlement is as large as it is, and the export tells you how many squares that is before you open it somewhere it will not fit.
  • Only forges are exported as lights. A forge is a fire and the simulation says so; nothing else in a CartoVox settlement is a light source, and a lantern hung on every door would be invented by the exporter rather than known. The rest is the referee's to place, and the export says so.
Honest about scope

Regional data guides local design. Exact street-level terrain and river banks are authored approximations, not simulated at that scale, and the food-supply report states planning assumptions rather than measured yields.

Scenes and encounter maps

Maps with no world behind them

A scene is a map that does not need a planet: a keep interior, a woodland encounter, a village square. Create them from the Library, and they get saved documents, versions, PNG and SVG rendering, portable backups and recoverable trash like everything else.

  • Canvas — square, landscape or portrait, with local author coordinates instead of geography, and square or hex encounter grids with your own colours, spacing and units.
  • Nested scenes — parent and child hierarchies up to eight levels, with breadcrumbs, child navigation and clickable hotspots that carry you from one scene to another. Export and import a whole connected tree.
  • Drawing layers — up to 32 named layers with saved opacity, Normal, Multiply or Screen blending, and non-destructive erase and restore masks that stay inside their own layer.
  • Lighting — Warm interior, Moonlit exterior and Dusk moods; add, drag, name and tune up to 32 lights; adjust overall darkness and shadow colour. It is authored atmosphere, and the app says so rather than implying simulated visibility.
  • Height and shadow 0.9 — tell a scene how tall each thing is and it casts a shadow. A wall, a tower, a fallen column, a table: give it a height in your scene's own feet or metres and it stands up, so a standing tower and a collapsed one stop looking the same. One sun for the whole sheet, because that is what makes a page read as having depth — twelve drop shadows set by hand that disagree about where the light is do not, however deep each one looks. Twice as tall is twice as long a shadow, taken at life size and not stylised, so a wall shadows the rubble beside it and a narrow alley sits back from an open courtyard. What stands is what you drew: an unfilled rectangle is a room's walls and casts a wall's shadow, a filled one is a solid block, and if your floor and your walls are one filled shape the control says so by name and tells you how to separate them. Nothing tilts — every footprint stays exactly where you drew it, the grid still measures, and a token still stands on the square it is on. It is off until you ask, and the sheet says in plain words that the heights are yours rather than measured.
  • Art directions — Illustrated adventure, Engraved parchment or Clear tabletop applied to palette, linework, lettering, markers and atmosphere as a single Undo step, previewed before it touches the document.
  • Original vector kits — village exteriors, stone fortifications, terrain and landscape, and rooms and furnishings: cottages, longhouses, workshops, shrines, market stalls, wells, gardens, carts, curtain walls, towers, gatehouses, bridges, groves, boulders, reed ponds, fords, beds, tables, hearths, bookcases, chests and rugs.
  • A shared library — save any artwork or assembled structure to an on-device catalogue with previews, names and tags, reuse it in any world or scene, and share it as an art pack.
  • Drawing tools — Select, Stamp, Path/wall, Area, Brush and Erase with a contextual inspector; Bézier paths with editable handles; groups nested eight deep; snapping with alignment guides; resize and rotate on canvas; align and distribute.
  • Reference images — drop a full-detail PNG or JPEG beneath the artwork, or calibrate one onto a whole-world sheet by matching two image landmarks to two world locations.
  • One 40-step Undo across world maps and scenes: artwork, captions, layers, masks, lettering, appearance, recipes, canvas and output settings all follow the same labelled timeline.

Write → Codex

Codex and lore

Measured geography, suggested lore and your own decisions in one place. Generated social history is a proposal until you accept or rewrite it; everything you write survives re-simulation.

  • Seventeen sections — Overview, Questions this world raises, Your canon, Import lore, Lands, Gazetteer, Realms & geopolitics, Cultures, History, Faith questions, Trade & economy, Places of power, Story hooks, Naming studio, Rename everything, Describe everything, Physical dossier.

  • Rename everything 0.9 — every named thing in the world on a single editable sheet: realms, cultures, faiths, places of power, settlements. A canonical name propagates through maps, dossiers, lore, routes, tongues, exports and later revisions.
  • Describe everything 0.9 — the prose of the world in one place: every realm's government, motto, strength and weakness, its ruler's title, character and secret; every culture's diet, burials and taboos; most of every faith; and cities and towns, whose descriptions are read back in the dossier, the vault and search. Measured findings are left alone: a place of power's kind is read off the simulation, so the sheet does not offer to edit it. Write → Chronicle

Chronicle

  • Geological Deep Time and the human-scale Chronicle are kept separate. Initialize from Lore timeline imports the generated history as editable eras with stable links to their source entries; Add authored era starts from scratch; Refresh Lore events pulls in later lore edits.
  • Eras carry a title, date or age, summary and a base map (relief, realm territories or others). Events are points, routes or areas — war or battle, migration, border change, settlement change, route or expedition, disaster, political and other — with persistent consequences that remain visible after the era in which they began.
  • Play steps through the eras on the map; the legend explains the symbols; the scrubber names the era and its date.
  • Chronicle maps, campaign maps, regional projects and lore maps share one annotation model, so a marker made in one is understood by the others.

Read before you lean on a number

Known limits

CartoVox documents what it approximates, at length, inside the app. The short version:

  • Desktop is the authoring target. The mobile layout reviews a world; it does not build one.
  • Drawing density is not simulation detail. 2×–4× and Render Studio interpolate the same saved cells; only a full-resolution rebuild adds simulated cells, and it does so in a separate project.
  • The default tectonics is one kinematic snapshot, correlated around generated ridge systems. Deep Time transports crust for hundreds of millions of years but does not solve mantle convection.
  • Four seasons, not twelve months; Köppen thresholds are applied per season. Orbital cycles are forcing, not an N-body integration. Tides are climatological, not a tide table.
  • No thermohaline circulation — currents are wind-driven only. Erosion runs two passes, so valleys look young. Hydrology is regional: a directed reach graph with lake nodes and morphology classes, not fluid dynamics.
  • Soils are regional pedology; resources are prospects, not proven reserves; glacial history is reconstructed, not integrated through every ice age.
  • Land needs crust to stand on. Asking for more land than there is continental crust drains the sea until seafloor is exposed, and the run says so.
  • Atlas labels are placed, not typeset: a name that will not fit at any rung of its shrink ladder is dropped and reported rather than broken over two lines. Stamps are procedural; mountains read as massifs rather than chains; sub-cell coastline crinkle is a deliberate, switchable invention.
  • A long thin landmass near the pole is usually the projection, not the terrain — measure it on the sphere.
  • Reproducibility is checked two ways: the same seed in two processes must give byte-identical plates, and a seed must render identically whether or not another world was drawn first.
  • Publication cost depends on the selected outputs. A Full build with every plate currently takes about 8 minutes on a high-end Apple Silicon Mac and about 13 minutes on a high-end Windows machine; the preflight measures your own machine as it goes.

Version-by-version model changes are recorded in the release notes, each versioned in the world's configuration so a saved world always states which model produced it.
